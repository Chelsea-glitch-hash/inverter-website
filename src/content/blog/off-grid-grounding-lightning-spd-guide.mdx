Grounding rarely gets the engineering attention that sizing and battery
selection do, yet it decides two things a buyer actually cares about: whether
the system is safe to touch, and whether it survives its first lightning
season. The same handful of mistakes repeat in the field — a floating AC
neutral that makes every RCD trip at random, an equipment ground that was
never run, a PV array bonded to nothing, a single surge protector asked to
defend an entire compound. None of these show up at handover. All of them
show up eventually. This guide separates the three jobs that get lumped
together as "grounding", then covers bonding, array practice and layered
surge protection for rural off-grid sites.

## Three different jobs called "ground"

Most grounding confusion starts because one word covers three distinct
functions. Keep them separate in your head and in your wiring:

1. **DC return.** The negative conductor that carries load current back to
   the battery. It is a circuit conductor, not a safety electrode, and in
   a standard negative-ground system it carries the full load current all
   day. It belongs in the cable, sized for voltage drop.
2. **Equipment grounding (bonding).** The network that connects every
   exposed metal surface — inverter chassis, array frames, racking,
   battery enclosure, combiner boxes, the AC distribution box — into one
   continuous conductor system. Under normal operation zero current flows
   here. Its job is to provide a low-impedance fault path so that a wire
   touching metal trips the protective device instead of energizing the
   cabinet someone opens.
3. **The earth electrode system.** Ground rods and their interconnections,
   which give the whole installation a reference to earth and a path for
   lightning and surge energy to dissipate into soil.

The safety-critical rule that follows: **the earth electrode and the
equipment bonding network normally carry no current.** If you measure
steady current on a grounding conductor, something is wrong — almost always
a wiring error or a missing neutral-ground bond, not a property of the
system.

## Build the bonding network first

Before any electronics are powered, every metallic enclosure and frame in
the installation should be bonded to a common point — typically a ground
bus bar in or beside the equipment enclosure — using conductors sized per
local code (6 mm² to 16 mm² copper is a common range for these runs; your
code decides). From that bus, one heavy conductor runs to the earth
electrode.

Practical points that separate a real bonding network from a nominal one:

- **Bond the array structure.** Module frames and mounting rails are the
  largest metal surface on the site and the most exposed to sky. Use the
  module manufacturer's designated grounding points and serrated or
  toothed bonding washers that bite through anodizing; anodized aluminum
  is an insulator until scratched.
- **Bond across every enclosure gap.** A hinged metal door on a combiner
  box, or an inverter mounted on a painted bracket, needs an explicit
  bonding jumper. Paint and powder coating are insulators.
- **One common point, radial runs.** Bond conductors should fan out from
  the single ground bus rather than daisy-chaining enclosure to
  enclosure, so a single loose connection cannot silently disconnect
  everything downstream.
- **Continuity is a test, not an assumption.** A few ohms from any module
  frame back to the ground bus is the expectation; an open circuit on a
  run that "was connected" is a common finding after a season of
  vibration and corrosion.

## The earth electrode: rods, resistance and soil

The electrode is what the entire protection chain dumps into, so it
deserves more than one rod kicked into dry dirt.

- **Depth beats count.** Soil resistance falls as rods reach moist,
  compacted strata. A single rod driven deep is usually better than two
  short ones; if multiple rods are used, space them at least about twice
  their driven length apart and bond them together.
- **Target resistance by function.** A common working target is under
  roughly 10 ohms for general earthing; values in the single digits are
  preferable where lightning exposure is high. Dry, sandy or rocky soil
  can make the target hard — deep-driven rods, multiple rods, conductive
  backfill or a buried ring around the building are the standard
  remedies. Measure with an earth tester rather than assuming.
- **Bond the electrode into one system.** Every rod, the water well
  casing if metallic, and any structural steel at the site should be
  tied into one equipotential network. Separate, unconnected electrode
  systems are how dangerous voltage differences appear between "the
  pump" and "the house" during a strike.
- **Keep corrosion couples in mind.** Copper in direct contact with
  galvanized steel in wet soil corrodes the steel. Use bimetallic
  connectors or keep the copper above grade in its own conductor run.

## One neutral-ground bond — wherever the source is

The AC side has its own famous rule, and it is worth restating because it
is the single most common cause of mystery RCD trips in off-grid
installations: **an AC system has exactly one neutral-to-ground bond, and
it must exist at the active source.**

In a grid-tied house, the utility supplies the bond and the electrician
never thinks about it. Off-grid, the bond must be created by whichever
source is live:

- An **inverter-charger** typically closes an internal relay that bonds
  neutral to ground while it is inverting from battery, and opens that
  bond while passing external AC through, so the incoming source's bond
  becomes the reference.
- A **generator** may be shipped with its neutral bonded to the frame —
  in which case it must be the only bond while it powers the system, and
  the inverter's internal bond must be inactive during passthrough.

If no bond exists, the AC neutral floats: outlets still show voltage, but
sensitive loads misbehave and RCDs trip erratically because the
protection scheme has no valid reference. If two bonds exist at once,
load current leaks onto the grounding conductor and every RCD reads it
as a fault. Both conditions are wiring fixes, and both are verified the
same way: with the system running from each source in turn, measure
between neutral and ground at an outlet. Expect roughly 0 V; tens of
volts means a floating neutral, and persistent RCD trips with valid
voltage usually means a double bond. For a deeper treatment of the
generator side — auto-start, AC input limits and the transfer case — see
our [generator integration guide](/blog/off-grid-generator-integration/).

## PV array practice: bonding and insulation

Two array-level decisions shape the grounding design:

- **Floating or grounded DC?** Most modern off-grid inverters and MPPT
  charge controllers use transformerless or high-frequency stages that
  require a **floating PV array** — neither positive nor negative DC
  conductor may be bonded to ground. Bonding a DC pole on such equipment
  trips ground-fault alarms or damages the unit. Grounded-array designs
  still exist, mainly on some older and utility-scale equipment, but you
  must follow the equipment manual, not a habit from another system. The
  array *frames* are bonded to the equipment grounding network in both
  cases — frame bonding and DC-conductor bonding are different things,
  and only the second one is prohibited on floating systems.
- **Cable entry and insulation resistance.** Water ingress in connectors
  and junction boxes drives the classic "alarm after rain" PV
  insulation fault. Route cables so they cannot form drip loops into
  connectors, use matched connector systems from one manufacturer, and
  keep a record of the array's insulation resistance at commissioning so
  seasonal degradation is visible against a baseline.

## Lightning: what actually protects the site

Rural off-grid sites are often the tallest conductive object for
kilometers, which makes them lightning exposure points whether or not
anything is officially installed. The engineering picture is more
nuanced than "add a ground rod":

**An air terminal does not absorb a strike.** A lightning protection
system (air terminals, down conductors, its own dedicated electrode
field) gives a strike a preferred, controlled path. It coexists with the
low-voltage grounding system but keeps its own conductors, joined at
grade per the lightning protection standard your market follows. What
destroys electronics is rarely the direct strike itself — it is the
induced surge from a strike tens or hundreds of meters away, coupled
into long conductors. That is what SPDs are for.

**Surge protection is layered, not single:**

| Location | Device | What it stops |
|---|---|---|
| AC distribution panel | Type 2 (or Type 1 where exposure is severe) AC SPD | Surges arriving on or induced into the AC wiring — utility lines, long generator cabling |
| PV input, at or near the inverter/controller | DC SPD rated above maximum array open-circuit voltage | Induced surges from the array's long outdoor DC runs |
| Communication lines (BMS links, monitoring, Ethernet) | Data-line SPD | The surge entry point most installs forget entirely |

Three installation facts decide whether those devices work:

1. **Lead length is everything.** A surge protector works by clamping and
   diverting; every centimeter of conductor between the protected line,
   the SPD and ground adds inductance, and a strike-level current across
   that inductance leaves hundreds of volts *in addition* to the SPD's
   clamping voltage. Mount SPDs directly at the entry point, keep leads
   short and straight, and land them on the bonding network, not on a
   private wire to a distant rod.
2. **The whole path must be rated.** An SPD on a panel whose ground run
   is a thin, corroded wire does almost nothing. The divert path is the
   product: electrode, bonding network, SPD — sized and connected as one
   system.
3. **SPDs die doing their job.** Thermal-disconnect designs degrade with
   each surge absorbed. Choose devices with visible end-of-life
   indication, inspect them on a maintenance schedule, and treat a spent
   SPD as a system that has already protected you once — replace it.

**Equipotentialization is the quiet half of lightning design.** During a
nearby strike, everything rises together; equipment is destroyed by
*differences* in potential between two bonded systems, or between a
conductor entering a building and the metalwork inside it. That is why
the PV array, the well pump, the generator and the house steel all
belong on one bonding network — and why cables that cross between
buildings (pump power, guest-room feeders) deserve SPDs at both ends or
a routed path that keeps their systems bonded.

## Common field failures and their grounding causes

| Symptom | Likely cause | First check |
|---|---|---|
| RCD trips randomly, no load pattern | Floating AC neutral (no active bond) | Measure N-G at an outlet while each source runs |
| RCD trips when generator runs | Double neutral-ground bond | Verify one bond per source mode |
| PV ground-fault alarm after rain | Connector or junction-box moisture | Insulation resistance test per string |
| Inverter or MPPT killed every storm season | Missing/undersized DC or AC SPD; long lead length | Inspect SPD windows and lead runs |
| Tingles from pump frame and inverter case simultaneously | Two unbonded electrode systems at different potentials | Bond the structures into one network |
| Grounding conductor warm or carrying current | Wiring error or missing bond | Trace current path — electrode must be near-zero current |

## Commissioning checklist

- [ ] Bonding continuity measured from module frames, inverter chassis, battery enclosure and AC panel back to the ground bus
- [ ] Earth electrode resistance measured and recorded
- [ ] Exactly one neutral-ground bond verified in each source mode (inverting and generator/passthrough)
- [ ] PV array floating where the equipment requires it — no DC pole bonded to ground
- [ ] AC SPD at the distribution panel, DC SPD at the PV input, both with short, straight leads to the bonding network
- [ ] Data/communication lines (BMS, monitoring) surge-protected
- [ ] SPD end-of-life indicators green at handover
- [ ] Baseline insulation resistance of the array recorded for future comparison
- [ ] All of the above photographed and archived in the handover file

The last two items are the ones buyers skip and later wish they had not:
a baseline insulation reading turns "the alarm came back" into a
one-visit diagnosis, and photos of SPD status are the difference between
a warranty claim and a debate.
