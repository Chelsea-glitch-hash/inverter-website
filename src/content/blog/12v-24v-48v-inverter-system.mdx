The system voltage — 12 V, 24 V or 48 V — is decided early in an off-grid
design and is hard to change later. It trades cable size and efficiency
against component cost and availability.

## The basic trade-off

Power is voltage × current. At a fixed power level, **higher voltage means
lower current**, and lower current means thinner, cheaper cable and less loss.

| System | Typical battery | Inverter range | Cable size for same power |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 V | Single battery | 300–1,500 W | Thick, expensive |
| 24 V | Two batteries | 1–3 kW | Medium |
| 48 V | Four batteries (or LiFePO4 pack) | 1–11 kW | Thinner, cheaper |

## Practical rules of thumb

- **12 V** — small loads under ~1 kW: cabins, camping, small boats, portable
  setups. Component availability is broad, but cable must be heavy.
- **24 V** — medium loads 1–3 kW: small homes, workshops, RVs. A good
  middle ground for compact systems.
- **48 V** — anything above ~3 kW: full homes, telecom, farms, businesses.
  Cable costs stay manageable and higher-power inverters are abundant.

## Why 48 V wins for bigger systems

Above roughly 3 kW, a 12 V bank needs very heavy cable (often 2× 000 AWG for
a 5 kW system) and wastes a meaningful share of energy in line losses. 48 V
keeps currents low — a 5 kW inverter at 48 V draws about 104 A, which is
comfortably handled by normal-size cable.

## Matching your inverter

Pick the inverter **first**, then build the bank to its DC input voltage —
not the other way around. Every product page on this site lists the DC input
range in the specification table, so the system voltage is confirmed before
