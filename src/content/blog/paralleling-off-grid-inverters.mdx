A single inverter only goes so far. When a project outgrows the biggest model in
a series — or when the budget says "start smaller now, expand later" — the
practical answer is often to run two or more identical inverters in parallel.
Done properly, a parallel bank behaves like one much larger unit; done badly,
it makes smoke, trips faults, and costs weeks of service time.

This guide explains what paralleling really involves: the hardware that must
match, how master–slave coordination works, the wiring rules that decide
whether load is shared evenly, and a commissioning sequence that catches
mistakes before they become failures.

> **First, the honest caveat:** not every inverter supports paralleling, and
> the limits differ per series. Always confirm with the manufacturer that a
> given model supports parallel operation, the maximum number of units, and
> the exact wiring and settings before you design around it. Nothing in this
> article replaces the manual for your specific unit.

## When paralleling makes sense

Three scenarios come up again and again in B2B projects:

- **Phased expansion.** The customer starts with one unit for today's load and
  adds a second (or third) identical unit when demand grows. One cable and
  communications link later, the bank is bigger — without replacing hardware.
- **Inventory simplicity.** A distributor can stock a single power class and
  cover 1×, 2× or 3× projects with the same SKU instead of warehousing several
  different models.
- **Service redundancy.** With a properly configured bank, a failed unit can
  be isolated and replaced without taking the whole site offline (check your
  platform's fault behavior — some stop the entire system on a unit fault).

## What can and cannot be paralleled

The single most common mistake is mixing hardware. A parallel bank works
because every unit produces a waveform that stays locked to one shared
reference; that only works when the units are electrically and logically
identical.

- **Same model, same series** — required. Mixing different models, power
  classes or even hardware revisions is not supported on mainstream platforms.
- **Same firmware version** — strongly recommended; a bank running mixed
  firmware can fail the synchronization handshake or drift apart under load.
- **Same DC system voltage** — all units must share the same battery bank
  voltage (for example all 48V).
- **A dedicated synchronisation link** — the master coordinates phase and
  frequency over a communications cable between every unit. This is *not* the
  battery communication cable; it is the heartbeat that keeps AC outputs in
  phase. Missing or loose, the units refuse to parallel — or worse, attempt it
  and circulate current between each other.

The typical supported range for residential-class hybrid and off-grid
platforms is between 2 and 16 units, with the practical ceiling usually set by
battery current and busbar sizing rather than the electronics. Our own
[off-grid inverter range](/products/off-grid-inverters/) spans 900W to 5000W,
and the [off-grid solar inverter range](/products/off-grid-solar-inverters/)
reaches 11kW — the parallel support, if any, is specific to each series and
must be confirmed before quoting a multi-unit design.

## Single-phase parallel vs split-phase stacking

People use the word "paralleling" for two different topologies, and confusing
them causes wiring mistakes:

- **True paralleling** stacks units on the same phase to add power on that
  phase — three 5kW units on the same output give a 15kW single-phase bank.
- **Split-phase stacking** assigns two units to opposite legs (L1 and L2) to
  build a 120/240V split-phase supply. Some platforms do both: two units per
  leg across two legs for a high-power split-phase system.

Decide which topology the project needs *before* wiring, because the phase
assignment in the unit menu must match the physical wiring exactly. On a
split-phase build, verify rotation and leg assignment with a meter before
connecting any loads.

## Master–slave coordination and addressing

One unit is designated the **master**; the others follow. This is usually set
in each unit's menu or with DIP switches, and the addressing rules are strict:

1. Set every unit to the parallel-enabled mode.
2. Assign exactly one master and give each unit a **unique address** —
   typically the master is unit 1 and slaves are numbered 2, 3, 4…
3. The master generates the reference waveform and broadcasts it over the
   synchronisation link; slaves lock to it cycle by cycle.
4. On many platforms the master also talks to the battery BMS and the energy
   meter, so the measurement point lives in one place — do not wire metering
   to every unit or the readings fight each other.

Skip any of these steps and the bank either refuses to start or runs with
uneven, unstable output.

## DC side: one battery bus, wired symmetrically

The DC side is where most parallel-system problems are born. Rule of thumb:
**all units connect to one common battery bus, and every cable to every unit
is the same length, type and cross-section.**

Why symmetry matters: a battery cable carries the full bank current, and if
unit A's cable is half a metre shorter than unit B's, its lower resistance
lets it source more current and work harder. The fix is not thicker cable — it
is *equal* cable to every unit, landed on a common busbar rather than
daisy-chained unit to unit.

A quick sanity number: a 5kW inverter on a 48V bank draws roughly 104A at
full load, so a three-unit bank can pull more than 300A from the battery.
Size the busbar and cables for the combined current, fuse each unit
individually, and torque every lug to the manual's specification.

Two battery strategies exist:

- **Shared battery bank** — simplest, cheapest, one BMS conversation. The
  whole bank draws from one pool of capacity.
- **Independent banks per unit** — more redundancy (one bank's fault does not
  take out the others) but more equipment and individual management. Only
  valid if the platform supports disabling battery sharing.

## AC side: common load bus, one ground bond

On the AC side, backed-up loads connect to a **common load busbar**, with
individual protection for each unit's output. The key safety rule for any
multi-inverter site: **the neutral–earth bond exists in exactly one place.**
With several units, confirm that only the master (or the transfer point) holds
the bond — two bonds in a parallel bank create circulating ground currents and
dangerous voltage imbalance.

Generator input, where fitted, should be connected so every paralleled unit
sees it, and phase rotation must match the inverter's programming.

## The commissioning sequence that prevents smoke

Bringing up a parallel bank adds steps to the normal startup:

1. **Bring up each unit alone first** and confirm it runs solo without errors.
2. Power everything down, connect the synchronisation cables, and set the
   master/slave roles and addressing.
3. Power up the **master first**, then the slaves.
4. With the bank live and **no load**, verify every unit reports the same
   frequency and voltage.
5. Apply a test load and check the split — two units should each carry roughly
   half, not 80/20. An uneven split points to a sync or settings problem, not
   a healthy bank.
6. Leave the site under observation for the first full day of real loads; an
   imbalance that hides at idle often shows up when a workshop or household
   hits its morning peak.

## When not to parallel

Paralleling adds cost, wiring and failure modes. If a single unit covers the
load with headroom for surges, or if the project genuinely needs three-phase
output, a properly sized single unit (or a three-phase platform) is usually
the better call. Parallel banks earn their keep when the project is
**future-scaling by design** — today one unit, tomorrow two or three.
