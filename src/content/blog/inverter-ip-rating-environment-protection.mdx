Two of the most common early-failure stories in off-grid solar have nothing to do with sizing or wiring. In one, an indoor-class inverter is bolted to an exterior wall because the utility room was full, and its terminals start corroding within two rainy seasons. In the other, a sealed outdoor unit is buried behind a hot metal shed, starved of airflow, and quietly derates itself every afternoon until the electronics cook. Both failures trace back to the same under-read specification: the IP rating on the datasheet.

This guide explains what the IP code actually protects against, how humid, coastal and dusty environments attack an inverter in different ways, and how to match enclosure rating, mounting location and maintenance practices so the hardware survives its site. This is the enclosure-environment side of inverter selection; the thermal side is covered separately in our guide to [power derating at high temperature and altitude](/blog/inverter-power-derating-temperature-altitude/).

## What the IP code actually measures

IP stands for Ingress Protection, standardized in IEC 60529. The code always has two digits, and each digit answers a different question. The first digit, from 0 to 6, describes protection against solid objects and dust. The second digit, from 0 to 9, describes protection against water. Higher numbers always mean tighter protection, and the two digits are independent: an enclosure can be dust-tight but barely splash-resistant, or vice versa.

For inverters and PV balance-of-system hardware, five ratings cover nearly every product you will encounter:

| Rating | Solids protection | Water protection | Practical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| IP20 | Fingers and tools (objects over 12.5 mm) | None | Indoor-only, dry electrical rooms |
| IP21 | Fingers and tools | Vertical dripping water only | Indoor with minor condensation tolerance |
| IP54 | Dust-protected, small ingress allowed | Splashing water from any direction | Semi-sheltered outdoor under an eave or ventilated cabinet |
| IP65 | Fully dust-tight | Low-pressure water jets from any direction | Fully exposed outdoor mounting, direct rain |
| IP66 | Fully dust-tight | Powerful water jets from any direction | Exposed coastal and monsoon-grade installations |

Two reading rules prevent most misinterpretations. First, an IP21 unit is not a weatherproof product; it tolerates the occasional condensation drip from a cold ceiling, not rain. Second, IP65 and IP66 protect against water jets, not submersion; a unit in a flood-prone pit is mis-specified regardless of its rating.

## How each environment attacks the enclosure

Different climates kill inverters through different mechanisms, and the countermeasures differ accordingly.

**Humidity and condensation** are the quiet killers. When an enclosure sits in a humid region and the temperature swings between day and night, moisture condenses on the cooler internal surfaces — typically overnight, when the electronics are off and cannot self-dry. Repeated condensation cycles leave a conductive film on circuit boards, corrode terminals and degrade insulation. An inverter that runs daily in a warm room dries itself simply by operating; one that sits idle through humid nights and cold dawns accumulates moisture instead.

**Salt fog** near the coast is more aggressive than ordinary humidity. Airborne salt particles settle on any surface the air reaches, and salt combined with moisture forms a mildly conductive, corrosive film. Within a few years, unprotected terminals inside a nominally intact cabinet can show rust and green corrosion while the housing looks fine from outside. Marine-grade practice treats salt exposure as a permanent coating problem: sealed enclosures, sealed cable entries, and periodically re-checked connections.

**Dust and sand** combine with airflow. Any inverter that cools itself with an intake fan — which includes most fan-cooled low-frequency units — pulls ambient air, and everything suspended in it, across its heatsinks. Dust alone reduces cooling efficiency; dust mixed with humid air forms a conductive sludge that both blocks fins and can bridge circuit-board traces. Fine sand in desert regions abrades and works into every unprotected gap.

The worst outcomes come from combinations: dust plus humidity in tropical monsoon regions, salt plus storm-driven spray on islands, dust plus condensation in high-day-night-swing deserts. Specify for the combination, not just the headline climate.

## Matching rating to mounting location

The practical decision is not "which IP number is best" but "where will this unit physically live." The mounting location determines the required rating — not the other way around.

**Dry, ventilated indoor rooms** (plant rooms, technical baselines, enclosed equipment cabinets) are the natural home for IP20 or IP21 hardware. Indoor-class enclosures often ventilate better and cost less, and that is a genuine advantage when the room itself does the protecting. Many of our [off-grid inverters](/products/off-grid-inverters/) with aluminum-alloy housings and temperature-controlled fans are installed this way, where the room keeps dust and moisture away from the intake vents.

**Semi-sheltered outdoor spots** — under a deep eave, inside a ventilated but roofed enclosure, on a covered veranda wall — need at least IP54. The unit stays out of direct rain but still faces splash, blowing dust and daytime heating.

**Fully exposed outdoor walls and rooftops** demand IP65 as the working minimum, with IP66 preferred where wind-driven rain, heavy monsoon seasons or hose-down cleaning are realistic. At this level, cable gland sealing becomes part of the rating: an IP65 unit with an unsealed knockout hole is effectively not IP65.

**Coastal and marine air** shifts the question from rating to material and sealing detail. IP66 helps, but the meaningful checks are stainless or coated fasteners, sealed terminals, conformal-coated boards where the manufacturer offers them, and a mounting position out of direct spray. For island and remote-coast projects where service visits are expensive, over-specifying the enclosure is usually the cheaper choice.

A frequent and expensive mistake is buying correctly rated equipment and then mounting it in the wrong place — for example, an indoor-class unit on an exposed wall because the electrician had space there. The rating label describes the enclosure; the installation decides whether that enclosure is adequate.

## The thermal trade-off behind sealed enclosures

Higher ingress protection is not free. An IP65 or IP66 enclosure cannot have open ventilation slots, so it rejects heat through its case and heatsink fins by conduction and natural convection. That works well for sealed outdoor string inverters but constrains how much power the same volume can dissipate.

Fan-cooled inverters sit at the opposite end. Intake fans move far more air and support higher continuous power in a smaller box — but every intake is an opening, which is exactly why fan-cooled units are typically rated IP20/IP21 and belong indoors or in filtered cabinets. Our fan-cooled off-grid models use intelligent temperature-controlled fans that only spin when the heatsink demands it, which limits how much dust-laden air is pulled through the unit; in dusty rooms that still means periodic cleaning of intakes and fins.

The selection logic follows directly: if the site is dusty and the unit must be sealed, expect a physically larger or more heavily finned enclosure for the same power, and verify the manufacturer's high-ambient performance rather than assuming the nameplate holds. If the site allows a clean indoor room, a ventilated indoor-class unit is often the more robust long-term choice, not a compromise.

## Installation practices that decide survival

Even a correctly rated unit fails early when installed carelessly. These practices cost little and prevent most enclosure-related failures:

- **Choose the wall before the wall bracket.** Pick the driest, most temperature-stable location the site allows; every step of exposure you avoid is protection you do not have to buy.
- **Leave airflow clearance.** Mount with 10–15 cm of clear space on all sides so convection works; never flush-mount against an uninsulated wall that soaks up afternoon sun.
- **Avoid heat traps.** A metal shed directly above the unit collects and radiates heat; shade helps, sealed hot pockets do not.
- **Seal every cable entry.** Use proper glands or filler plugs on all knockouts. One open hole defeats the entire rating.
- **Elevate in flood-prone sites.** Keep electronics above realistic water lines; no IP rating covers submersion.
- **Orient drip loops.** Route cables so water runs down the cable and drips off before it reaches the gland.
- **Breathe or dehumidify sealed cabinets.** In humid climates, a vent plug or small desiccant pack in an outer cabinet limits internal condensation cycling.

## Maintenance and inspection checklist

Enclosure protection degrades over time, and the failures are gradual enough to catch early. Fold these checks into routine visits:

1. **Inspect seals and glands** at every service: perished gaskets, loose glands and cracked cable entries are the main re-entry paths for moisture.
2. **Clean intakes and fins** on fan-cooled units in dusty rooms; a clogged heatsink quietly raises operating temperature long before any alarm.
3. **Look for early corrosion** at terminals and fasteners — green or white deposits on connectors mean salt or humidity is getting in, and the connection should be cleaned, re-treated and re-torqued.
4. **Check for condensation evidence** inside transparent display windows or outer cabinets; recurring droplets indicate the mounting microclimate is worse than assumed.
5. **Re-verify after storms.** Driving rain and flooding find the weak entry points; inspect after every severe weather event rather than waiting for the annual service.

## Commissioning checklist for enclosure and environment

Before handing over the system, confirm the environment side is actually as designed:

- The installed IP rating of the unit matches the actual exposure of its mounting location (indoor, sheltered, exposed, coastal).
- All cable entries are sealed, and unused knockouts are plugged.
- Airflow clearance meets the manufacturer's minimum on all sides, and no heat-trapping structure sits directly above the unit.
- Drip loops are formed on all cable runs entering the enclosure.
- In coastal or high-humidity sites, a corrosion inspection date is scheduled for the first service visit rather than the annual one.
- The owner knows which cleaning and inspection tasks are theirs between services.
