An inverter can be rated comfortably above your total running load and still
shut down the moment a pump, refrigerator or air compressor tries to start.
The reason is surge power — the brief but enormous current an electric motor
draws in the first fraction of a second. Sizing for surge is the single most
common gap between a spec sheet that looks right and a system that works in
the field.

## Why motors trip an "adequate" inverter

An induction motor does not simply switch on. Before it reaches operating
speed, it behaves like a near-short-circuit: the rotor is stationary, so the
windings see maximum slip and draw **locked-rotor current** — typically 3 to 7
times the running current, lasting from a few tenths of a second (small fans)
to 2–3 seconds (loaded pumps and compressors).

Two more factors stack on top:

- **Power factor.** At startup the motor's power factor collapses toward
  0.3–0.5, so the apparent power (VA) the inverter must supply is far higher
  than the running watts suggest.
- **Voltage sag.** While the surge flows, the inverter output voltage dips.
  If the sag is deep enough, other electronics on the same circuit may reset,
  and the inverter itself may interpret the event as a short circuit and latch
  off.

A resistive load — a kettle, an oven, incandescent lighting — never does any
of this. That is why two systems with identical running watts can behave
completely differently in the field.

## The two numbers on every inverter spec sheet

Inverter capacity is always stated twice, and both lines matter:

| Spec | What it means | Typical figure |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous (rated) power | AC watts the unit can deliver indefinitely at `25°C` | The headline number, e.g. 3000W |
| Peak / surge power | Short-duration overload, usually 5–30 seconds | Commonly 2× rated; low frequency designs can reach 3× or more |

Three rules for reading these figures honestly:

1. **Check the surge *duration*, not just the multiplier.** A "2× for 5
   seconds" rating handles a light pump but not a deep-well pump with a long
   ramp-up. A rating quoted without a duration is close to meaningless.
2. **Surge is not additive headroom for continuous use.** It exists to cover
   motor starting only. Never plan to run a load continuously inside the
   surge band.
3. **Surge capability depends on the DC side too.** The inverter can only
   source the surge if the battery bank and cabling can deliver it. A
   3000W inverter on a small 12V battery with thin cables will trip on surge
   even if the unit itself could have managed it — at 3000W a 12V bank must
   supply roughly `250A` (`3000 ÷ 12 × 1.1` efficiency correction), while the
   same load on 48V draws about `69A`.

## The loads that surge

Most household and workshop failures trace back to a handful of inductive
loads. Typical figures for common single-phase equipment:

| Load | Running watts | Typical startup draw |
|---|---|---|
| LED lighting, laptop, TV | 10–150W | 1× (no surge) |
| Refrigerator / freezer | 100–300W | 3–6× for about 1 second |
| Ceiling fan | 60–120W | 2–3× briefly |
| 0.5 HP surface water pump | ~400–750W | 3–5× for 1–2 seconds |
| 1 HP submersible pump | ~1000–1500W | 3–6× for 1–3 seconds |
| Air compressor (2 HP shop class) | ~1500W | 4–6× for 2–3 seconds |
| Split-type air conditioner (1 ton) | ~900–1200W | 4–6× compressor start |
| Power saw / angle grinder | 800–1500W | 2–3× on load engagement |

These are engineering rules of thumb, not datasheet values. Whenever the
motor nameplate or the pump datasheet publishes a **locked-rotor amperage
(LRA)** figure, use it: `surge watts ≈ LRA × voltage`. If only horsepower is
given, a 1 HP single-phase motor at 230V draws roughly 6–8A running and can
momentarily pull 40A or more at start.

## A worked sizing example

Scenario: a rural clinic with a vaccine refrigerator, lighting, a laptop
charging station and a 0.75 kW submersible water pump.

**Step 1 — running load.** Refrigerator 200W + lighting 150W + laptops 200W
+ pump 750W = `1300W` of loads that could overlap. Add 25% margin →
`1625W` continuous minimum.

**Step 2 — worst-case surge.** The dominant event is the pump starting
*while* everything else runs. Pump surge at 4× running: `750 × 4 = 3000W`,
plus the other 500W of steady loads → `3500W` momentary demand.

**Step 3 — match to the spec sheet.** You need an inverter whose
*continuous* rating covers step 1 (≈ 2000W) and whose *surge* rating covers
step 2 (3500W for up to 2 seconds). A 2500W unit with a 2× (5000W) surge
spec clears both numbers; a 2000W unit with an undocumented "peak" figure
is a gamble.

**Step 4 — verify the DC side.** At 24V, the surge current is roughly
`3500 ÷ 24 ≈ 146A`. The battery bank, main fuse and battery cables must all
be rated for that momentary current — which is one reason 48V is the
practical choice for systems at this size and above.

## Design levers when surge exceeds the budget

If the honest surge calculation lands above your budget, resist the urge to
just buy a bigger inverter. Several cheaper levers exist:

- **Stagger the starts.** Most trips happen because two motors start at once.
  A timer or a simple operating rule ("pump and air conditioner never share
  the minute") can halve the surge requirement.
- **Fit a soft starter to the biggest motor.** A soft-start module on the
  compressor or pump limits inrush to roughly 2× running current. On a deep
  well pump this is often the difference between a 3kW and a 6kW inverter.
- **Move up in system voltage.** At the same watts, doubling battery voltage
  halves DC current, reduces cable heating and lets the inverter hold its
  output voltage steadier through the surge. See our guide on
  [choosing 12V, 24V or 48V](/blog/12v-24v-48v-inverter-system/) for the
  full trade-off.
- **Consider low frequency topology for pump-heavy sites.** The transformer
  in a low frequency design absorbs surge more gracefully than a compact
  high frequency unit — the detailed comparison is in our
  [low frequency vs high frequency guide](/blog/low-frequency-vs-high-frequency-inverter/).
- **Oversizing the PV array does not help.** Array watts charge the battery;
  they do not appear at the AC output during a sub-second surge. Sizing the
  array is a separate exercise covered in
  [our panel-count worksheet](/blog/how-many-solar-panels-for-off-grid/).

## Commissioning check: test the worst case

Do not discover surge limits after handover. Before site acceptance, run the
single hardest event the system will ever see: start the largest motor while
every other major load is on. Watch for:

- Does the inverter ride through the start without tripping?
- Do the lights dip noticeably? (A brief, shallow dip is normal; a flicker
  that resets electronics means DC cabling or battery capacity is marginal.)
- Does the battery voltage at the inverter terminals stay above the low-DC
  cutoff during the start?

Fifteen minutes of deliberate worst-case testing prevents most field callbacks.
