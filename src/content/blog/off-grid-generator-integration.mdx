Solar and batteries carry an off-grid system most days, but every serious
off-grid design still assumes a generator is somewhere in the picture. A
week of monsoon cloud, a winter of short days, or a load that grew after
handover — the generator is what converts those from "system failure" into
"fuel cost". Yet generator integration is also where many otherwise good
installs go wrong: an undersized genset that stalls when the charger kicks
in, auto-start logic that short-cycles the engine to death, or a grounding
arrangement that trips every RCD in the building. This guide covers the
sizing, configuration and commissioning decisions that make the pairing
work.

## What the inverter's AC input actually does

A pure DC-AC inverter has no generator input at all — it only converts
battery power outward. An off-grid **solar inverter** (inverter-charger)
has an AC input terminal that does two things when it detects a valid
source:

1. **Passes the AC through** (or closes an internal transfer relay) so the
   generator powers the load panel directly, and
2. **Charges the battery bank** from that same source through the internal
   charger.

This dual role drives every sizing rule below, because the generator is
never just powering the loads and never just charging — it is doing both at
once, at whatever power the charger has been configured to draw.

## Sizing rule: charger demand plus concurrent loads

The most common integration failure is pairing a small generator with a
large inverter-charger at default settings. The charger is configured to
pull, say, 60A AC — and a 3 kW genset stalls on the first charge cycle.

Work the number honestly:

`generator kW ≥ (charger AC watts ÷ power factor) + concurrent AC loads + 20% headroom`

A worked example: a 48V battery bank charged at 40A DC during bulk phase
draws roughly `48 × 40 = 1920W`, plus charger losses gives about 2100W of
generator output consumed by charging alone. If the evening loads —
lighting, a refrigerator, a water pump — run 1000W while the generator is
on, the genset must deliver at least `2100 + 1000 = 3100W` continuous. With
20% headroom, the honest spec is a **4 kW continuous** generator, not the
"4 kVA peak" sticker on a machine that sustains 3 kW.

Two habits that prevent chronic undersizing:

- **Use the continuous rating, never the peak.** A "7 kVA" generator that
  sustains 5.5 kW is a 5.5 kW generator for this purpose.
- **Account for loads running during charging.** In practice the generator
  is most often started in the evening when loads are on. Sizing for
  "charging only" guarantees overload trips at exactly the wrong hour.

There is a second, quieter benefit to adequate size: a well-loaded
generator runs efficiently, but a heavily *over*loaded one sags in voltage
and frequency. If the AC input drifts outside the inverter's acceptance
window, the inverter rejects the source and the system oscillates between
generator and battery — the classic "genset runs but nothing charges"
complaint.

## Choosing the generator type

| Generator type | Output quality | Best fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inverter (variable-speed) genset | Clean sine wave, tight frequency regulation | Small systems, top-up charging, noise-sensitive sites | Usually capped at 2–4 kW continuous |
| Conventional petrol | Acceptable, but voltage/frequency vary with load | Mid-size installs, cost-driven markets | Higher THD; keep sensitive electronics on the inverter side |
| Diesel | Stable under sustained load, best fuel economy per kWh | 5 kW+ systems, daily or heavy seasonal runtime | Weight, servicing, fuel storage rules |

Two electrical facts matter more than brand. First, **frequency stability**:
the inverter's AC input typically accepts a narrow window around nominal
frequency (roughly ±2 Hz on many units), and a conventional genset under a
sudden load step can swing wider than that, triggering rejection. Second,
**waveform quality**: high total harmonic distortion from a cheap genset
can confuse the inverter's input detection just as thoroughly as a frequency
drift. Inverter-type gensets sidestep both issues, which is why they dominate
small installs despite their price.

## Configure the AC input current limit

Every serious inverter-charger lets you set an **AC input current limit** —
the maximum the unit will draw from the generator for charging plus
passthrough loads. This is not an optional trim; it is the mechanism that
matches the charger to whatever genset is actually on site:

- A 4 kW continuous generator at 230V single phase delivers about
  `4000 × 0.8 ÷ 230 ≈ 14A` safely usable after the 80% continuous-load
  derating.
- With 1000W of expected concurrent loads (`1000 ÷ 230 ≈ 4A`), set the
  input limit near `14 − 4 = 10A`, leaving roughly 2.3 kW for the charger.

If the charger's maximum setting exceeds that, the inverter simply charges
more slowly — which is exactly the right behavior. The alternative (no
limit) is a stalled or tripping genset every bulk-charge cycle.

Also set the **charger's charge profile** to match the battery bank — the
voltage and current limits for lithium (usually communicated over BMS
link) differ from sealed lead-acid three-stage charging. The generator
integration is a good moment to verify this, because charging from a
genset happens at higher, sustained currents than a cloudy-day solar
trickle.

## Auto-start: thresholds that keep the engine healthy

Most off-grid solar inverters and system controllers can start the
generator automatically through a dry-contact or CAN/RS485 command when the
battery runs low, and stop it when the charge is done. Sensible defaults:

- **Start trigger** — state of charge around **20–30%** (raise to 35% in
  cold climates, where usable capacity shrinks), or the low-DC voltage
  equivalent.
- **Stop trigger** — **80–90% SoC**. Do not chase 100% with the generator:
  the last 10% of a charge cycle is the slowest, and letting the sun finish
  the bulk-to-absorption tail is free.
- **Minimum run time** — **30–60 minutes**. Without it, a battery that
  crosses the start threshold briefly causes start/stop/start churn that
  wears the starter motor and never completes a full charge stage.
- **Quiet hours** — block auto-start overnight where noise rules or sleeping
  occupants apply, unless the battery genuinely cannot hold until morning.

One wiring note that saves hours of debugging: run the generator start
signal wire (typically low-voltage twisted pair) **separately from the AC
power conductors**. Shared conduit couples switching noise into the start
circuit and produces phantom start attempts.

## One neutral-ground bond, only one

Grounding is the most misunderstood part of generator integration, and the
failure mode is loud: every RCD/GFCI in the building trips the moment the
generator takes over.

The rule: **an AC system must have exactly one neutral-to-ground bond, at
the active source.** Many generators are shipped with their neutral bonded
to the frame. Many inverter-chargers contain an internal transfer relay
that bonds neutral to ground while inverting from battery, and opens that
bond while passing external AC through. If both bonds are active at once,
load current splits between the neutral conductor and the ground conductor,
and the RCD reads the difference as a ground fault.

Three workable arrangements:

1. **Floating-neutral generator** feeding an inverter-charger whose internal
   bond relay handles the transfer — the cleanest default, and the standard
   configuration for systems designed around an inverter-charger.
2. **Bonded generator + inverter with the internal relay configurable** —
   disable the inverter's bond while external AC is present, so the
   generator's bond becomes the single reference during generator hours.
3. **A transfer scheme that switches the neutral** — necessary when neither
   device manages the bond dynamically.

Whichever arrangement applies, verify it rather than assume it: with the
system running from the generator, measure between neutral and ground at an
outlet. Expect roughly 0V. A reading of tens of volts indicates a floating
neutral (no active bond); RCD trips indicate a double bond. Both are
wiring fixes, not equipment faults — and both are far cheaper to fix during
commissioning than during a warranty call. Local electrical code governs
the details; treat the above as engineering practice, not a code citation.

## Commissioning checklist for the pair

- [ ] Generator continuous output documented, AC input current limit set to match
- [ ] Charge profile matches the installed battery chemistry
- [ ] Full-load test: generator carries charging *and* expected concurrent loads for 30+ minutes without tripping
- [ ] Auto-start trigger verified by forcing the SoC/voltage condition
- [ ] Minimum run time set; start/stop cycle observed once end to end
- [ ] Neutral-ground bond present exactly once in each source mode (inverter mode *and* generator mode)
- [ ] RCDs hold under both sources
- [ ] Frequency/voltage acceptance window respected at the genset's worst load step

The full-load test is the one that finds real problems. A genset that runs
clean at no load and stalls at 80% load is a sizing error the customer
would otherwise discover during the first rainstorm.
