Every inverter carries a number on the box: 3000W, 5000W, 11kW. But that
number was measured under laboratory conditions — typically 25°C ambient, at
sea level, with free airflow around the unit. Put the same inverter inside a
ventilated telecom cabinet in a 45°C climate, or on a rooftop site at 3000 m
elevation, and the honest answer to "how much power can it actually deliver
all day?" is usually less than the nameplate says.

This matters most for B2B buyers, because undersizing shows up in the field as
tripped overloads, premature fan failures and angry end customers. This guide
explains the three forces that shrink real power — temperature, altitude and
airflow — how to read a derating curve, and how to size a system that still
delivers its rated watts when the site gets hard.

> **A note on numbers.** The derating figures below are typical industry
> patterns drawn from common datasheet behavior, not specifications of any
> single model. Always size from the actual derating curve printed in the
> datasheet of the unit you intend to buy — if a manufacturer will not publish
> one, treat that as a red flag.

## Why nameplate watts are measured in a lab, not in your project

Standards bodies define a reference condition for inverter ratings: 25°C
ambient temperature, sea level, unobstructed air movement, and often a
specific DC input voltage. At that reference point the unit can deliver its
continuous rated output indefinitely.

Field conditions diverge from that reference in predictable ways:

- **Ambient heat** raises the temperature of the power semiconductors, which
  shortens their life and forces the firmware to back off output to protect
  them.
- **Altitude** thins the air, so the heat sink and fans move less heat away per
  second.
- **Enclosed or stacked installation** blocks airflow and traps the inverter's
  own waste heat.
- **Low battery voltage or PV input near the edges of the window** also trims
  deliverable power, though that is a separate derating axis from the thermal
  ones this article covers.

None of this means the manufacturer is cheating. It means a rating is a
statement about a reference condition — and your job, as a buyer or designer,
is to translate the nameplate number into a real-site number.

## Temperature derating: the most common power loss

Power semiconductors and magnetic components generate heat that scales roughly
with output power. An inverter designed for a certain internal junction
temperature simply cannot hold full output when the surrounding air is already
hot, because there is less temperature difference left to push the waste heat
out.

A typical derating pattern seen across off-grid and residential hybrid
inverters looks like this:

| Ambient temperature | Available continuous output (typical pattern) |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| 25°C (77°F)         | 100% of rated                                   |
| 40°C (104°F)        | 85–95% of rated                                |
| 45°C (113°F)        | 75–85% of rated                                |
| 50°C (122°F)        | 60–75% of rated                                |
| 55°C (131°F)        | 50–65% of rated                                |

Two patterns are worth knowing so you can sanity-check any datasheet:

1. **Full power up to a "thermal knee".** Many units hold 100% output up to
   40°C and only start tapering above it. A datasheet that quotes
   "continuous power at 40°C" is telling you where that knee is.
2. **Linear taper above the knee.** Beyond the knee, output typically drops in
   a straight line with temperature until the unit reaches its maximum rated
   ambient and shuts down protectively. The slope tells you how much a hot
   climate will cost you.

For a tropical project — think Nigeria, India, Thailand or the Gulf — the
relevant figure is usually the 40–45°C column, not the 25°C one.

## Altitude derating: thin air carries less heat

Heat sinks and fans cool by moving air. At higher elevation the air is less
dense, so each cubic metre of airflow carries less heat away, and the fan does
more work for less cooling. For that reason most inverters are certified and
rated at or below 2000 m, and derate above it.

The typical rule seen in datasheets: above 2000 m, expect roughly **0.5–1% of
output lost per additional 100 m** of elevation. That translates to:

- At 2500 m: roughly 97–100% of rated output.
- At 3000 m: roughly 90–95% of rated output.
- At 4000 m: roughly 80–90% of rated output — check the specific model.

For high-altitude projects — Andean, Himalayan, East African or central
Mexican sites — pair the altitude figure with the temperature figure, because
mountain sites are often both high *and* hot during the day, and the two
deratings compound multiplicatively rather than additively.

## Cooling design: what the manufacturer does about all this heat

Derating is a safety valve, but good thermal design pushes the knee higher so
the valve rarely has to open. When you evaluate a unit, look at how the heat
gets out:

- **Fan-controlled cooling.** Most units use temperature-controlled fans that
  stay off (or slow) at low load and speed up as the heatsink warms. Every
  model in our [off-grid inverter](/products/off-grid-inverters/) line ships
  with an intelligent temperature-controlled fan — it keeps idle noise down and
  protects components during sustained output.
- **Heatsink and housing material.** Aluminium housings spread heat across a
  larger surface. Some of our [off-grid inverter](/products/off-grid-inverters/)
  models use an aluminium alloy case explicitly for heat dissipation.
- **Fan failure detection.** A unit that cannot tell you its fan has failed is
  a unit that will silently derate — or overheat — until someone notices.
  Prefer models with over-temperature protection and fault reporting.
- **Derating curve published.** The single best procurement signal: the
  manufacturer publishes a temperature/altitude derating table in the
  datasheet. If it is missing, ask for it.

Installation practice matters as much as hardware. Leave the manufacturer's
recommended clearance around the unit, keep vents unobstructed, avoid stacking
units directly above each other, and never mount an inverter so that its own
exhaust blows into another unit's intake.

## Continuous vs surge: two numbers, both real

While we are on the subject of "which watts count", separate the two ratings
an inverter actually has:

- **Continuous rating** — the power the unit can deliver hour after hour at a
  stated ambient temperature. This is the number that derates.
- **Surge (peak) rating** — the power available for a few seconds to start
  motors, compressors and pumps. Surge capability is largely independent of
  thermal derating because it is short-lived, but it is also finite: check
  both the multiple (often 2–3× continuous for high-frequency units, more for
  low-frequency designs) and the duration.

Size the continuous load against the *derated* continuous figure at your
site's worst-case ambient, and the starting surge against the surge figure.
Mixing the two up is how systems that "look fine on paper" trip on day one.

## A quick worksheet: turning rated watts into site watts

For a first-pass estimate, multiply the nameplate by the two thermal factors
and any power-factor allowance:

**Real continuous power ≈ Rated × temperature factor × altitude factor**

Worked example — a 5000W off-grid inverter in a ventilated enclosure at a
site that hits 45°C and sits at 2200 m:

- Temperature factor at 45°C (typical mid-range pattern): ~0.80
- Altitude factor at 2200 m (200 m above the 2000 m threshold, at ~1%/100 m):
  ~0.98
- Real continuous power ≈ 5000 × 0.80 × 0.98 ≈ **3920W**

So the design should treat this unit as roughly a 4 kW continuous source on
that site — even though the box says 5000W. If the project's peak continuous
load is 4.5 kW, this unit is undersized, and the honest fix is a bigger model,
a cooler location, or both.

The same logic applies up the range: our [off-grid solar inverter
line](/products/off-grid-solar-inverters/) tops out at 11kW at the reference
condition, and a 10.5kW or 11kW model placed in a hot roof cavity will deliver
meaningfully less during the hottest hours — size the array, the loads and the
inverter so the *derated* number clears the load, not the nameplate number.

## B2B checklist: questions to ask before you quote

When you are buying inverters to resell or install at scale, add these to your
qualification list:

1. **What is the continuous output at 40°C and 45°C?** If the answer is "the
   same as at 25°C", confirm that with a printed curve — some units genuinely
   hold full power to 40°C, others simply omit the bad news.
2. **What happens above the maximum ambient rating?** Clean thermal shutdown,
   or repeated tripping that will frustrate end users?
3. **At what altitude does derating start, and by how much per 100 m?**
4. **What clearance and ventilation does the installation manual require?**
5. **Is there a temperature sensor on the heatsink, and is there fan-fault
   protection?**
6. **Does the unit report its internal temperature** (via display or remote
   monitoring) so a hot site can be caught before failures start?

Buying on the nameplate alone is how distributor stock turns into field
failures. Buying on the derating curve is how a project keeps its promises in
July — and at 3000 m.
