Every off-grid inverter will alarm eventually. A cold morning pushes the
battery voltage low, a dusty fan saturates the heatsink, a pump start sags
the output for half a second — and the display shows a code nobody wrote
down. The units that come back to the dealer with five-star reviews are not
the ones that never alarm; they are the ones whose owners recorded the code,
checked the obvious external causes first, and performed one controlled
restart instead of five panicked ones. This guide is that sequence,
organized so a site operator or installer can work through it without
guessing.

## A fault code names a condition, not a broken part

This is the single most useful thing to internalize: an inverter alarm
reports a **protection condition the unit detected** — battery voltage
outside a window, input frequency drifting, load current above a limit,
temperature above a threshold. It does not confirm that any component has
failed, and most alarms in the field resolve after correcting something
*outside* the inverter.

A battery-low alarm, for example, can trace to any of these:

- the battery genuinely discharged (weather, added loads),
- voltage sag across an undersized or corroded battery cable,
- a BMS protection action on a lithium bank,
- charge parameters set for the wrong battery chemistry.

None of those is an inverter defect. Treating the code as a diagnosis
instead of a symptom is what produces the classic support ticket: "inverter
shows fault, replaced under warranty, new unit shows the same fault."

Code numbers themselves are **not standardized across manufacturers or even
product families**. Always read them against the manual for your exact
model and firmware revision — the same two-digit code can sit in different
families on different units.

## Step 1: Record the fault snapshot before touching anything

The snapshot is what turns a mysterious shutdown into a solvable event.
Write down, or photograph the display for:

- the exact code, any text beside it, the LED pattern and audible alarm;
- time of day, weather, and whether PV was producing;
- battery chemistry, displayed battery voltage, state of charge, and any
  BMS indicator;
- which loads were running — especially pumps, compressors, heaters and
  chargers;
- the state of PV input, AC/generator input, and external breakers;
- anything that changed recently: new loads, rewiring, a storm, a service
  visit.

If the inverter has logging or a monitoring portal, preserve the alarm
history before any power cycle. Repeated resets overwrite exactly the
pattern a support engineer needs, and a unit that arrives at the dealer
with its history wiped gets treated as a mystery fault.

## Step 2: Sort the alarm by family

Nearly every off-grid inverter alarm falls into one of six families. The
family tells you where the first safe check goes:

| Alarm family | Usually points to | First safe action |
|---|---|---|
| Battery / BMS | Discharged bank, voltage sag at a connection, wrong charge settings, BMS protection | Check displayed battery voltage and BMS status before any reset |
| PV input | Array voltage out of range, wiring or polarity issue, low insulation | Do not reset; isolate PV and inspect, escalate suspected insulation or polarity faults |
| AC output / load | Starting surge, sustained overload, a shorted appliance or branch circuit | Shed nonessential loads (motors and heaters first) before one permitted restart |
| Temperature / cooling | Blocked airflow, high ambient, sustained heavy load | Let the unit cool; fix external ventilation and clearance |
| AC input / generator | Source voltage or frequency outside the acceptance window, unstable source | Restore a stable source; verify input settings match the generator |
| Internal / communication | Control-board or firmware-level condition | One restart if the manual allows; if it repeats, contact service — do not keep cycling |

Two families deserve a hard rule of their own. Suspected **PV insulation
or polarity faults** should stop at the isolate-and-escalate step: a wet,
damaged or arcing DC array is a safety event, not a convenience reset. And
**temperature alarms** are almost always an installation problem — the
fix is clearance and airflow, not a fan upgrade or a "better" inverter.

## Step 3: Inspect externally, safely

Before attempting any reset, do a walk-around with your eyes and nose:

- Look for smoke, burnt odor, melted insulation, water ingress, damaged
  connectors, or a swollen battery case.
- Check that ventilation openings are unobstructed and the unit has its
  rated clearance.
- Inspect accessible breakers, fuses, disconnects and cable lugs for trips,
  corrosion or heat discoloration.
- Confirm which protective devices have operated — an RCD or main breaker
  that tripped is information about *where* to look.

If you find visible damage, moisture inside an enclosure, exposed
conductors, arcing marks, or an unexplained protective-device trip, stop
here. Isolate the system and bring in qualified service. No restart
sequence outranks a physical hazard.

## Step 4: One controlled restart, loads shed

If the external checks are clean and the manual permits a restart, do it
once, deliberately, in this order:

1. Switch off nonessential AC loads, starting with motors, heaters, pumps
   and any appliance chargers.
2. Isolate PV and AC/generator input at their disconnects if the manual
   instructs it.
3. Power the inverter down and wait out its internal discharge interval
   (capacitors hold charge; the manual states the wait).
4. Restore the battery first and confirm a normal startup indication.
5. Restore PV and AC input, one source at a time where the design allows.
6. Reconnect loads gradually — small steady loads first, high-inrush
   equipment last.

The point of shedding loads first is discrimination: if the unit starts
clean and then alarms when a specific appliance comes on, you have found
the trigger. If it alarms immediately with nothing connected, the fault is
internal or in the DC side, and now you know that too.

## When not to reset at all

Repeated power-cycling is not troubleshooting; it is evidence destruction
with a side of risk. Do not attempt a reset if:

- there is any sign of burning, arcing, water ingress or battery swelling;
- the alarm is a PV insulation, ground-fault or reverse-polarity condition;
- the alarm has already recurred after one clean restart;
- the battery repeatedly hits a protection threshold with no obvious cause
  (a failing cell can hold displayed voltage but collapse under load);
- the unit alarms during startup before any load is connected.

These cases go to the supplier with the snapshot from Step 1 attached. The
photo of the display and the list of active loads cut days off the
diagnosis.

## Reading intermittent alarms by pattern

Intermittent faults are the hardest category, because the pattern itself is
the diagnostic. Match what you observe to the likely direction:

| Observed pattern | Likely investigation direction |
|---|---|
| Alarm appears after rain or heavy dew, clears by afternoon | Moisture entry: connector seals, cable low points, junction boxes |
| Alarm coincides with a specific appliance starting | Inrush/surge on a marginal unit, or voltage sag from undersized wiring |
| Alarm occurs only during generator hours | Source voltage/frequency drift outside the acceptance window |
| Alarm follows sunrise on cold mornings | Cold-boost PV voltage near the maximum, or a low-SoC battery sagging |
| Alarm migrates with a particular PV string | String-level insulation or connector problem |
| Alarm recurs with no pattern at all | Log it longer, check firmware revision, escalate with the history |

The rain pattern deserves emphasis because it is so common and so often
misread as "the inverter is faulty." Insulation resistance that drops when
everything is wet and recovers when dry is an array-side issue — find the
wet path, do not reset through it.

## Prevention: settings documentation and the alarm log

Two habits prevent most repeat alarms and make the remaining ones cheap:

- **Document the configuration.** Battery type and charge parameters, AC
  input limits, low-voltage cutoff, generator acceptance settings — record
  them at commissioning and keep the sheet with the unit. A large share of
  "faults" traced by service teams are settings mismatches: a lithium bank
  charged on a lead-acid profile, an input limit set above what the genset
  sustains, a cutoff voltage tuned for the wrong battery count.
- **Review the alarm history quarterly.** Trends show up before failures:
  temperature alarms creeping earlier each summer, battery-low alarms
  arriving sooner each winter. Both point at drift you can fix on a
  scheduled visit instead of an emergency one.

For installers handing systems to customers, add a one-page "what this code
means, what to check, when to call us" card to the handover pack. It
prevents the repeated-reset behavior that destroys the evidence trail.
