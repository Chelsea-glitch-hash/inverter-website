Low frequency and high frequency describe how an inverter converts DC to AC,
and the distinction decides surge handling, weight and long-term reliability.
Both are sold for off-grid use — the right answer depends on your loads.

## How they differ

**Low frequency inverters** use a heavy copper-wound transformer at mains
frequency. The transformer provides natural surge capacity and electrical
isolation, and the units are built around a simple, proven topology.

**High frequency inverters** use high-frequency switching with ferrite
transformers. They are lighter, smaller and often more efficient at partial
load, but their surge capability depends on the electronics rather than a
physical transformer.

## Comparison

| Aspect | Low frequency | High frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Surge capacity | Excellent (5–8× rated briefly) | Moderate (2–3×) |
| Weight | Heavy (transformer) | Light |
| Cost | Higher | Lower |
| Efficiency | Slightly lower at light load | Often higher |
| Idle consumption | Higher | Lower |
| Typical use | Pumps, tools, large motors | Home backup, electronics |

## When surge matters

Induction motors — water pumps, compressors, power tools — draw a large
inrush current when starting, often 3–7 times their running watts. A low
frequency inverter's transformer absorbs this surge more gracefully, which is
why pumps and workshop equipment favor low frequency designs.

For general home backup powering lights, computers and appliances, a high
frequency inverter is lighter, cheaper and more efficient, and its surge
rating is usually adequate.
