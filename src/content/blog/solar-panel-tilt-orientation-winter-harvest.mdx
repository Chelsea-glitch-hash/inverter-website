Two off-grid systems can carry identical inverters, identical batteries and
identical panel counts — and one of them runs the generator all December
while the other barely notices winter. The difference is usually not the
equipment. It is where the glass points. Electrical sizing gets the
attention because it involves the expensive components, but the array's
orientation and tilt decide how much of the sun's energy ever reaches the
MPPT input in the first place. A mis-aimed array is a permanent, silent
loss: no fault code, no alarm, just fewer amp-hours every single day for
twenty-five years.

This guide covers the four placement decisions that matter for off-grid
and hybrid systems — azimuth, tilt, row spacing and multi-direction
mounting — with the practical angle rules that matter most when the
design goal is a reliable worst month, not a pretty annual total.

## Why off-grid optimization is a winter problem

A grid-tied array is judged on annual production: every kilowatt-hour is
worth roughly the same, so the geometry that maximizes the yearly total
wins. An off-grid system is judged differently. Energy produced in summer
surplus is largely thrown away — the battery fills, the charge stage
throttles back, and the extra harvest has nowhere to go. Energy produced
in the worst month, by contrast, is the difference between autonomy and
generator hours.

That asymmetry has a direct design consequence: **optimize the array for
the worst month, and accept the summer penalty.** The same logic drives
worst-month array sizing (covered in
[our telecom site design guide](/blog/off-grid-telecom-site-power/)),
but sizing assumes the plane-of-array irradiation figure you feed it is
actually achievable. Geometry is what makes that number real — or not.

## Azimuth: point at true north or south, not the compass needle

The first decision is direction. In the northern hemisphere the array
should face true south; in the southern hemisphere, true north; within
about 10° of the equator, the optimum drifts and tilt matters more than
azimuth. The classic mistake is using an uncorrected compass: compass
needles point at magnetic north, which can sit 10–20° away from true
north depending on location. That error transfers straight into the
array.

How much does azimuth deviation actually cost? The losses are gentler
than most buyers fear, which is worth knowing before rejecting a roof:

| Azimuth deviation from due south | Typical annual yield retained |
|---|---|
| 15° | ~99% |
| 30° | ~96–97% |
| 45° | ~93–95% |
| 60° | ~88–89% |
| 90° (due east or west) | ~76–80% |

Two practical readings of that table. First, a 20–30° azimuth compromise
on an otherwise good roof is usually acceptable — spend the negotiation
effort elsewhere. Second, the losses grow steeply past 60°, and they grow
faster in winter than the annual figure suggests: the low winter sun
spends its short day in a narrow arc around the due-south azimuth, so a
west-facing array loses a disproportionate share of exactly the harvest
the system depends on. For off-grid work, treat due east or west as a
last resort, not a mild compromise.

Fixing the compass problem is trivial: use any phone's GPS-based compass
in true-north mode, or correct the magnetic declination for the site's
latitude and longitude.

## Tilt: the latitude rule and the winter adjustment

Tilt is the angle between the panel surface and horizontal. The
engineering shorthand is well established:

- **Fixed, optimized for the year:** tilt ≈ site latitude.
- **Optimized for winter:** tilt ≈ latitude + 15°.
- **Optimized for summer:** tilt ≈ latitude − 15°.

The annual optimum actually lands a few degrees below latitude in most
sunny regions, because strong summer direct radiation rewards a flatter
panel — but for an off-grid system the winter figure is the one that
matters. At latitude 40°, that means panels standing at roughly 55°,
which looks aggressive and works. The steeper tilt catches the low
winter sun near-perpendicular while the flat-mounted neighbor reflects
a large slice of it away as cosine loss.

Three second-order effects push the winter number even steeper, and all
of them favor the same adjustment:

- **Snow shedding.** In snow country, a steep array clears itself; a
  shallow one accumulates a white blanket that zero MPPT algorithms can
  recover. Where snow is a design case, steep tilt is not optional.
- **Dust and soiling.** The same self-cleaning logic applies in arid and
  agricultural regions: rain rinses a steep panel, dust cakes a flat
  one. Heavily soiled sites effectively add 2–5° to the optimum.
- **Ground mounts vs roofs.** Adjustable-tilt racking on a ground mount
  lets you run latitude + 15° in winter and latitude − 15° in summer,
  worth roughly 5–10% of annual energy over a fixed compromise — most
  valuable on small systems where a manual adjustment twice a year is
  cheap labor. Roof pitch is what it is; do not over-optimize a tilt you
  cannot change. The yield difference between a good and a perfect fixed
  angle is usually under a few percent, while shading and wiring
  decisions move the needle far more.

A worked comparison. A cabin at latitude 40° with a worst-month (December)
demand of 6 kWh/day needs, at 3.0 December peak sun hours and a 0.65
performance ratio, an array of about `6 ÷ (3.0 × 0.65) ≈ 3.1 kWp` —
*if* the irradiation figure is measured on the array plane. Feed the
sizing math the horizontal-surface irradiation instead, or mount the
array flat, and the same 3.1 kWp delivers maybe 2.2 kWh/day in December:
a system that is nominally correct and functionally short by a third,
with no component to blame. This is the failure mode that sends owners
shopping for "underperforming" inverters that were never the problem.

## Row spacing: the shading geometry nobody sketches

For anything larger than a small cabin system, panels end up in rows —
on a flat roof, a ground mount or a terraced hillside. Row spacing is a
geometry problem with an unforgiving failure mode: the front row shades
the back row precisely in the worst month, because the sun is lowest
when the loss hurts most. A system that performs evenly through summer
can lose half its December harvest to its own front row.

The standard design rule: the back row must be shade-free for at least
the hours around solar noon (typically 9 a.m. to 3 p.m.) on the winter
solstice. In practice:

`row spacing ≈ row height × shadow multiplier`

where row height is the vertical rise from the front row's bottom edge
to its top edge, and the shadow multiplier depends on winter sun
elevation at the site — roughly 2–3× at mid-latitudes (35–45°), and
larger further from the equator. Sketch it once with the solstice sun
elevation, then add margin. Shading losses on a partially shaded string
are not proportional: a shadow across a small corner of one module can
cut a whole string's output dramatically, because modules in series
share current. (Series behavior and its sizing consequences are covered
in [the MPPT voltage window guide](/blog/mppt-voltage-window-pv-string-sizing/).)

East-west mounting is the modern escape hatch for tight flat roofs: two
low-tilt rows facing opposite directions pack 30–50% more capacity into
the same area, nearly eliminate inter-row shading, and produce a flatter
"breakfast-to-dinner" generation curve that suits daytime-heavy loads.
The cost is 15–25% less total annual harvest per kilowatt compared with
due-south mounting, and a lower midday peak. For off-grid systems the
trade can still win — but verify it against the worst-month math, not
just the annual one, because low-tilt east-west rows are the geometry
most exposed to winter cosine loss.

## Multi-direction arrays on one MPPT: what mixes and what doesn't

Real roofs and real sites rarely offer one clean plane. The question
becomes: can strings facing different directions share an MPPT input?

The physics first. MPPT trackers find the operating voltage of whatever
is wired across them. Strings in **parallel** on one MPPT must share a
common operating voltage — which different orientations almost satisfy,
since tilt and azimuth move *current* far more than voltage. Strings in
**series** must share current, and mixed orientations in series drag
every module down to the weakest one's output: never do it.

So the rules that hold up in the field:

- **Same orientation, same tilt, same module type per string** — the
  non-negotiable baseline for parallel strings on one tracker.
- **Mixed orientations on one MPPT are workable** if every string
  satisfies the tracker's voltage window independently, because the
  current mismatch simply sums at the input. Expect the MPPT to find a
  compromise operating point slightly below the perfect-per-string
  optimum; on modest mixes (e.g. a south plane plus a small west wing)
  the penalty is usually a few percent.
- **Seriously different planes deserve separate MPPT inputs or a
  second charge controller.** An east-west pair, a ground array plus a
  roof array, or any mix with different module counts per string should
  each get its own tracker. Most mid-size hybrid and off-grid solar
  inverters already provide two MPPT inputs for exactly this reason.
- **Voltage windows still rule everything.** Each string, on each
  orientation, must pass the cold-Voc and hot-Vmp checks against the
  same MPPT range — a west-facing string sees different cell
  temperatures than a south one at the same hour.

## Commissioning checks for array geometry

Geometry mistakes are free to fix on paper and expensive to fix after
the racking ships. The checklist our engineers use before an array
design is released:

- [ ] Azimuth verified against true (not magnetic) north or south
- [ ] Tilt chosen for the worst month, with snow/soiling adjustments where relevant
- [ ] Sizing irradiation figures taken from the array plane (or corrected to it), not horizontal surface
- [ ] Row spacing sketched against the winter solstice 9 a.m.–3 p.m. sun
- [ ] Every string orientation passes cold-Voc and hot-Vmp against the MPPT window
- [ ] No mixed orientations in series; parallel mixes reviewed for voltage-window compliance
- [ ] Multi-plane arrays assigned to separate MPPT inputs where the mismatch is significant
- [ ] As-built azimuth/tilt recorded in the commissioning report — future diagnostics depend on it

The last item sounds administrative and is not. When a system
underperforms two winters later, the first diagnostic question is
"what is the array actually pointing at?" — and the answer should be in
the paperwork, not a guess.
