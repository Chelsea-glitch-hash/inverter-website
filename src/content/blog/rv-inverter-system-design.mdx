A cabin off-grid system lives in one place, gets sized once, and never
moves. A motorhome, caravan or campervan system is a different animal:
the "house" drives down the highway at 100 km/h, plugs into campground
shore power on some nights and nothing on others, and carries its power
sources with it — engine alternator, house batteries and a roof of solar
panels that changes angle every time the vehicle parks. The inverter
sits at the center of all of it, converting battery DC into the AC
power that the microwave, the air conditioner and the coffee maker
expect. Sizing and wiring that inverter for a moving vehicle is its own
discipline, and the mistakes people make are predictable enough to
write down. This guide walks through them in order.

## What makes an RV system different from a cabin

Four constraints shape every RV power decision:

1. **Three power sources, one load panel.** Shore power, the alternator
   and solar all feed the same house batteries. The inverter either has
   to coexist with shore power (through a transfer switch) or be
   switched out of the circuit when the campground pedestal is live.
2. **Every kilogram and every cable is mobile.** Wiring that is fine in
   a fixed house fails in a vehicle where vibration works connections
   loose and thermal cycling flexes crimps. Heavy cable runs and
   mounting quality matter more than in any static install.
3. **The battery bank is small and cherished.** A typical RV bank holds
   a few hundred amp-hours at most, so every watt the inverter burns
   idling at night is a watt taken from the morning's coffee.
4. **Loads are bursty.** An RV kitchen runs a microwave for 4 minutes,
   a kettle for 3, then nothing for an hour. This actually favors
   moderately sized inverters with strong surge ratings over one
   oversized unit running at a trickle.

## Step 1 — Audit the loads you actually run

List what runs *simultaneously*, not what exists. A realistic RV
inventory looks like this:

| Load | Running watts | Starting surge | Typical duty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential 12V fridge (via inverter) | 60–150W | 400–900W | Duty-cycled, 24h |
| Roof air conditioner (13,500 BTU) | 1,300–1,600W | 2,500–3,500W | Seasonal, hours at a time |
| Microwave | 1,000–1,500W | Slight, brief | Minutes |
| Coffee maker / kettle | 900–1,500W | None | Minutes |
| Induction cooktop (single burner) | 1,200–1,800W | None | Minutes to hours |
| Laptop, phones, router | 100–200W | None | Continuous |
| Entertainment / TV | 60–150W | None | Evening |
| Water pump (usually stays on DC) | DC load | DC load | Seconds at a time |

Two inventory rules save rework later:

- **Keep the vehicle's native 12V loads on DC.** Lights, water pump,
  furnace fan, slide-outs and the fridge (most models) are designed for
  the house battery directly. Running them through an inverter wastes
  10–15% in double conversion for no benefit. The inverter exists for
  the genuinely AC appliances only — this keeps it smaller and cheaper.
- **Note which loads are optional at the same time.** Air conditioning
  plus microwave plus kettle at once is a 4 kW+ demand; most RV owners
  simply learn not to stack them, which lets a 2–3 kW inverter cover a
  full-time lifestyle.

## Step 2 — Size the inverter

Add the running watts of everything on at once, then check the largest
motor or compressor against the surge rating:

`inverter watts = simultaneous running watts × 1.25 headroom`

Worked example: a couple full-timing without roof air runs the fridge
(150W), router and laptops (150W), TV (100W), and cooks dinner with an
induction burner (1,500W) while the microwave is off. Simultaneous load
≈ 1,900W, with headroom ≈ 2,400W — a **2,500W–3,000W** pure sine wave
inverter covers it with margin, and surge demand from the fridge
compressor starting against the other loads stays well within a typical
2× surge rating.

Adding a roof air conditioner changes the conversation: 1,600W running
plus 3,000W+ startup surge pushes the requirement past 4 kW continuous,
and — more importantly — past what most small battery banks can
discharge. Air conditioning on battery power is as much a battery and
current problem as an inverter problem.

## Step 3 — Respect the 12V current math

This is where most RV inverter plans break. The inverter's AC rating
translates into brutal DC current at low battery voltage:

`DC amps = AC watts ÷ battery volts ÷ inverter efficiency`

| AC load | Current at 12V | Current at 24V | Current at 48V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000W | ~93A | ~46A | ~23A |
| 2,000W | ~185A | ~93A | ~46A |
| 3,000W | ~278A | ~139A | ~70A |

At 2 kW on a 12V bank you are pulling nearly 200 amps — that demands
2/0 AWG or heavier cable, short runs, quality lugs and a fuse sized and
located correctly at the battery. Every connection becomes a heat
point. Practical thresholds, following the same logic as our
[system voltage guide](/blog/12v-24v-48v-inverter-system/):

- **Up to ~1,500W:** 12V is workable and matches the RV's native
  lighting and pump circuits.
- **1,500W–3,000W:** 24V halves the current and is the sensible choice
  for larger rigs; a small DC-DC converter feeds the legacy 12V loads.
- **Above 3,000W or with air conditioning:** 48V keeps cable cost and
  heat manageable, which is why the larger
  [off-grid solar inverters](/products/off-grid-solar-inverters/) in
  our range start at 24V and 48V banks.

Also verify the battery side: the BMS of each lithium battery has a
maximum discharge current, and a 200A draw may exceed what a single
100Ah unit can deliver. Pairing batteries and inverter is its own
subject — see [our compatibility guide](/blog/lithium-battery-inverter-compatibility/).

## Step 4 — Plain inverter or inverter-charger with transfer?

When the RV plugs into shore power, what happens to the inverter?

- **A plain inverter** must be switched off or isolated from the AC
  panel before shore power is connected — back-feeding an inverter from
  the grid destroys it. This suits small builds where the inverter
  feeds one or two dedicated outlets.
- **An inverter-charger with a built-in transfer switch** passes shore
  power through to the AC panel when the pedestal is live, and
  silently switches to battery mode when it is not. The same unit
  charges the house batteries from shore power in between. For any
  rig that visits campgrounds, this is the practical default — one
  box replaces an inverter, a charger and a transfer relay, and the
  switchover is fast enough that electronics ride through.

is exactly what our [off-grid solar inverter series](/products/off-grid-solar-inverters/)
provides in a single chassis: pure sine wave output, an MPPT solar
charger for the roof array, and an AC input that accepts shore or
generator power. For RVs that only need occasional AC for a laptop and
a TV, a standalone [pure sine wave inverter](/products/off-grid-inverters/)
from 900W upward — several of which are explicitly built for caravan

## Step 5 — Solar on a moving roof

Roof area is the hard limit: a typical van fits 400–600W of panels, a
caravan 600–1,200W. Two sizing realities differ from a fixed roof:

- **Orientation is never ideal.** Parked panels at random angles
  deliver a fraction of their rated power; plan on 50–70% of nameplate
  for a summer day and far less in winter or shade.
- **Compact MPPT range matters.** A motorhome roof mixes series strings
  constrained by clearance and shading from vents and air conditioners.
  Choose a charge stage whose MPPT window actually fits the array —
  our VM 1.5K, for instance, is a 12V-bank inverter-charger with a
  90Vdc–430Vdc MPPT window, which tolerates long rooftop strings and
  partial shading better than narrow-input units.

Solar rarely runs the air conditioner; its real job is to replace the
fridge, electronics and standby drain so the bank starts each evening
full. That pairing — a 1.5–3 kW inverter-charger on a 12V or 24V bank
with 400–1,000W of roof solar — covers the majority of real RV
lifestyles without a generator.

## Installation notes specific to vehicles

A mobile install adds requirements a fixed building never sees:

- **Mount on a rigid surface with vibration-rated fasteners**, thread
 locker on the brackets, and strain relief on every cable within a few
  centimeters of the terminal. Vibration loosens ring terminals slowly
  and then suddenly.
- **Ventilation clearance as specified by the manual**, and never in
  the same sealed compartment as the batteries — chargers vent and
  batteries must not share heat or stray gas.
- **Fusing at the battery end of every DC run**, sized to the cable,
  not the load. In a vehicle the fuse protects against chafed insulation
  finding chassis ground, which happens on the road, not on the bench.
- **GFCI protection on AC outlets**, especially in a galley or bathroom
  zone, per the electrical code that applies in your market.
- **Disconnects accessible without tools** — a battery switch between
  the bank and the inverter lets you de-energize the whole DC side for
  service or storage.

## Commissioning checklist for an RV inverter system

- [ ] Load audit documented; simultaneous-watts total within inverter rating with headroom
- [ ] DC current at full load calculated; cable gauge, lugs and fuse verified against it
- [ ] Battery BMS combined discharge current exceeds the worst-case inverter draw
- [ ] Surge test: largest motor load starts against the other running loads
- [ ] Shore-power transfer tested under load — no glitch that reboots electronics
- [ ] Charger stage profile matches the installed battery chemistry
- [ ] Solar: MPPT window verified against the actual roof string configuration
- [ ] All terminals torqued, thread-locked and strain-relieved; recheck after the first 1,000 km
- [ ] GFCI and disconnect operation verified
- [ ] Standby draw of the inverter measured and accepted against the overnight budget

That last item is easy to skip and worth doing: an inverter left on
overnight at 20–30W of idle draw consumes more daily energy than the
fridge it serves. If the unit has an eco or standby mode, learn when it
wakes up — and whether your clock or router tolerates the delay.
