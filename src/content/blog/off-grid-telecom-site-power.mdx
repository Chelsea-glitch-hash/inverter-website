A household off-grid system gets a second chance every morning: if the
batteries run low, the family turns something off. A remote telecom tower,
a microwave relay shelter, a pipeline monitoring station or a border
surveillance cabinet has no such luxury. The load is modest — often just a
few hundred watts of radio equipment, backhaul, controllers and security
devices — but it must stay online around the clock, in every season, with
nobody on site to shed demand or restart a tripped breaker. That single
constraint changes the entire sizing methodology. This guide walks through
the design decisions that separate a telecom power system that quietly runs
for years from one that generates monthly site visits.

## What makes an unattended site a different design problem

Three properties distinguish telecom and monitoring loads from residential
off-grid work:

1. **The load is continuous, not schedule-based.** Radio equipment draws
   its rated power 24 hours a day. There is no "evening peak" to design
   around because the peak *is* the average, and the daily energy figure
   is simply average watts × 24. A site averaging 600W consumes
   `0.6 × 24 = 14.4 kWh` per day — every day, in December as in July.
2. **Availability is contractual.** Operators typically specify uptime
   targets of 99.9% or better. The power system, not the radio, is almost
   always what breaches that number.
3. **Failed energy means failed service, not discomfort.** An undersized
   home system means candles; an undersized telecom site means dropped
   links, missed alarms, and a truck roll over roads that may be washed
   out precisely when the site is down.

The design consequence: telecom sizing is driven by the **worst month** of
solar resource and by **recharge recovery time**, not by annual averages.

## Step 1 — Build the load inventory from real data

For an existing site, log the load for at least a full week — longer if
cooling or traffic-dependent demand varies seasonally. For a new site,
build a 24-hour load plan from equipment datasheets:

| Load | Typical draw | Duty | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radio / baseband unit | 100–400W | Continuous | Varies with traffic and temperature |
| Microwave or fiber backhaul | 30–100W | Continuous | Include both ends' share if split |
| Router / switch | 10–30W | Continuous | |
| Security & lighting | 20–60W | Intermittent | Often DC, sometimes AC |
| Cooling / cabinet fans | 50–300W | Seasonal, duty-cycled | The wild card — measure it |
| Maintenance equipment | 500–2000W | Occasional | AC outlets for service visits |

Two rules that save rework later:

- **Keep DC and AC loads separate in the inventory.** Many telecom loads
  are natively DC and can be fed from the battery bus directly, which
  skips an inefficient conversion stage. The inverter only needs to carry
  the genuinely AC loads — cooling, tools, AC-powered auxiliary equipment.
- **Capture peak and inrush separately from energy.** Interval energy
  logging misses short surges. If the site includes AC cooling
  compressors or a pump, their starting surge sizes the inverter, not the
  daily kWh figure. (We cover motor-start sizing in detail in
  [our surge power guide](/blog/inverter-surge-power-motor-starting/).)

## Step 2 — Size the array on the worst month, not the average

The classic residential shortcut — annual average sun hours with a
derating factor — fails on unattended sites, because the worst month is
structurally worse than the average and there is no human load-shedding
to absorb the deficit. Size the array month by month:

`array kWp = daily energy kWh ÷ (worst-month peak sun hours × performance ratio)`

Use a performance ratio of **0.6–0.7** for a first pass on unattended
sites — it stacks temperature losses, dust and soiling, wiring and
conversion losses, battery round-trip loss and, critically in cold
climates, winter heating draw. Refine with site-specific data before
final design.

Worked example: a 600W continuous site needs 14.4 kWh/day. In a region
with a worst-month (December) plane-of-array figure of 3.5 peak sun hours:

`14.4 ÷ (3.5 × 0.65) ≈ 6.3 kWp`

Compare that with sizing on the annual average of 4.8 sun hours, which
would suggest only 4.6 kWp — a system that performs beautifully for nine
months and drops the link every December. If the site serves a latitudinal
or monsoon climate, checking every month's balance (not just the darkest)
is essential, because heating loads and snow soiling shift which month is
actually the constraint.

## Step 3 — Battery autonomy and the recharge window

Autonomy for a telecom site is not a comfort preference; it is the
difference between a cloudy week and an outage ticket. Sizing:

`bank kWh = daily energy × autonomy days ÷ (usable DoD × discharge-path efficiency)`

For the 14.4 kWh/day example with **3 days autonomy** at 80% depth of
discharge and 95% discharge efficiency:

`14.4 × 3 ÷ (0.8 × 0.95) ≈ 57 kWh`

Three telecom-specific considerations:

- **Autonomy replaces the generator on many sites.** Where fuel logistics
  are expensive or access is seasonal, extending autonomy from 2 to 3–5
  days of lithium storage often beats adding a genset — no fuel to haul,
  no engine to service, nothing to fail at 3 a.m.
- **Recharge recovery matters as much as autonomy.** After a deficit
  event, the array must both carry the load *and* replace the missing
  charge within one (at most two) good sun days. Verify the worst-month
  array surplus covers the deficit plus recovery; otherwise the battery
  slowly sinks into chronic undercharge and the BMS starts curtailing.
- **Low-temperature charging limits** apply to lithium banks in cold
  shelters. Either heat the battery compartment or let the BMS gate
  charging — never count on charge current the cells cannot accept at
  −10°C. (Battery-inverter pairing details are in
  [our compatibility guide](/blog/lithium-battery-inverter-compatibility/).)

## Step 4 — Choose the power architecture

Unattended sites converge on three architectures:

| Architecture | Fits | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure DC bus (no inverter) | All-DC loads, -48VDC equipment rooms | Fewest conversion stages, highest efficiency, standard telecom practice | AC maintenance loads need a small separate inverter |
| Battery-based off-grid inverter | Mixed DC + AC loads, 12–48V banks | One integrated unit: inverter, charger, transfer | Size for AC load surge, verify low-load stability |
| Solar pump/VFD-style dedicated units | Pumping or motor-dominant sites | Direct PV-to-motor efficiency | Not suited to mixed 24/7 loads |

For mixed-load shelters, a **battery-based off-grid solar inverter**
(our term for an inverter-charger with integrated MPPT) is the pragmatic
default: it converts battery DC to clean AC, charges the bank from PV,
and accepts a generator or weak grid on its AC input as a tertiary
source. The transfer between sources must be fast and clean enough that
radios and controllers ride through — which brings us to the
specifications that matter.

## Step 5 — Inverter specs that matter for unattended operation

Beyond raw wattage, evaluate against these criteria:

- **Continuous rating sized on the true AC load plus growth**, typically
  with 25–30% headroom. A site with 800W of AC load justifies a 1.5–2 kW
  inverter — not because it will ever run flat out, but because
  sustained operation near rating means high internal temperatures, and
  heat is the main aging mechanism in an enclosure that is visited twice
  a year.
- **Low-load efficiency and standby draw.** A 24/7 site spends most of
  its life at 20–40% of inverter rating. Check the efficiency curve and
  the no-load self-consumption; an inverter idling at 40W burns
  nearly 1 kWh/day — about 7% of our example site's budget — before a
  single radio watt is served.
- **Wide DC input window.** Banks sag and recover; the inverter must
  operate through the full charge and discharge voltage range of the
  chosen chemistry without nuisance trips. Our
  [multi-voltage off-grid inverters](/products/off-grid-inverters/)
  accept 12V to 72V DC input across the series, which covers both
  legacy 12/24V sites and modern 48V ones.
- **Surge tolerance** for cooling compressors, tools and any motor loads,
  per the motor-starting rules covered in
  [the surge sizing article](/blog/inverter-surge-power-motor-starting/).
- **Generator input acceptance** on narrow voltage/frequency windows for
  sites with backup gensets, with a configurable AC input current limit
  (see
  [generator integration](/blog/off-grid-generator-integration/)).
- **Environmental envelope.** Conformal-coated electronics, wide
  temperature ratings and temperature-controlled forced cooling for
  outdoor or shelter installs. Heat and humidity kill more remote-site
  inverters than lightning does.
- **Monitoring and remote alarms.** The site must report battery SoC,
  source status and faults over RS485/CAN or network uplink. An
  unmonitored off-grid site does not fail visibly — it fails on the day
  someone notices the link is down.

## Commissioning checklist for unattended sites

- [ ] Worst-month energy balance verified with monthly (not annual) irradiation data
- [ ] Recharge-from-deficit simulation: deficit forced, recovery time measured against target
- [ ] Full AC load + surge test passed with the smallest expected battery voltage
- [ ] Autonomy test: system holds load through a full simulated cloudy period
- [ ] Low-temperature charge behavior verified for lithium banks
- [ ] Standby draw measured and logged against the site energy budget
- [ ] Remote monitoring confirms SoC, alarms and source transfers end-to-end
- [ ] All protection and grounding per local code; SPDs installed on PV and AC sides
- [ ] Documentation pack left on site: single-line diagram, settings record, escalation contacts

The recharge test is the one most often skipped and most often needed.
An array that balances the load in steady state but cannot climb out of a
deficit turns the first bad weather week into a chronic brownout.
