Two inverters can both claim 94% peak efficiency and deliver completely
different runtime from the same battery bank. The peak figure is measured
at one favorable load point, usually around half of rated capacity. A real
off-grid system spends most of its life far below that point — overnight on
a few hundred watts, idle for hours with nothing switched on — and that is
exactly where the differences between designs are largest. Reading the
efficiency curve and the idle consumption figure correctly is the
difference between a battery bank that lasts the night and one that hits
low-voltage cutoff before dawn.

## One number is not a curve

Every inverter datasheet lists a maximum efficiency, and many buyers treat
it as a flat number. It is not. Conversion efficiency varies with the load
the inverter is carrying, and the shape of that variation follows a
consistent pattern across the industry:

- **Below roughly 10% of rated load**, efficiency drops sharply, because
  the inverter's own fixed overhead — control electronics, gate drivers,
  displays, fans — becomes a large share of the total power flowing
  through it.
- **Between roughly 30% and 70% of rated load**, efficiency reaches its
  plateau, typically 92–96% for a well-built unit.
- **Above 90% of rated load**, losses climb again as conduction losses in
  semiconductors and transformers grow with current.

The practical consequence: an inverter sized far above its actual loads
spends its whole life on the inefficient left end of the curve. A 5000W
unit powering 300W of evening lighting is converting at maybe 80–85%
effective efficiency — not the 94% on the datasheet.

## The load profile decides, not the peak

Compare two units serving the same 600W base load:

| Unit | Rated power | Load as % of rating | Typical efficiency at that point |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5000W | 12% | ~88% |
| B | 2000W | 30% | ~94% |

For identical loads, the smaller unit converts measurably better. The 6
point difference sounds abstract until you turn it into energy: at 600W
continuous, unit A burns roughly 43Wh per hour as heat while unit B burns
38Wh — and the gap widens further at lower loads, which is exactly where
an off-grid system sits overnight.

The right-sizing logic that follows from this is simple but often ignored:

1. **Audit the load profile first** — how many hours at each power level,
   not just the peak. A system whose overnight load is 300W should not be
   sized so that 300W is 5% of the inverter rating.
2. **Keep the dominant load band inside 30–70% of rating.** Short surge
   events still need headroom (see our surge sizing guide), but the *bulk
   of operating hours* should sit in the efficient middle of the curve.
3. **Do not buy rated watts for future loads that never arrive.** Every
   extra kilowatt of unused rating is paid for daily in idle draw and
   part-load losses.

## Idle and standby consumption: the load that never switches off

Separate from conversion efficiency is the power the inverter consumes
just to stay on. Datasheets call it idle draw, no-load consumption, tare
loss or standby power — read all of them as the same thing. Typical
figures:

- Small units (under 1 kW): roughly 5–15W
- Mid-range units (1–3 kW): roughly 10–30W
- Large low-frequency units (5 kW and above): can reach 30–60W, since the
  control and cooling overhead scales with the design

The number looks trivial next to a 3000W microwave. The problem is that it
runs 24 hours a day. The arithmetic:

- 20W idle × 24h = **0.48 kWh per day** — about 175 kWh per year,
  converted into nothing.
- On a small system with a 5 kWh usable bank, idle draw alone can consume
  close to 10% of daily generation before a single appliance runs.
- Overnight is worse in relative terms: from 18:00 to 07:00, a 25W idle
  draw plus 200W of phantom loads (standby electronics, chargers,
  detectors) can exceed the entire productive load of the evening.

This is one of the classic diagnoses behind "the batteries died overnight
and nothing was on": the inverter's own idle draw plus phantom loads
drained the bank while the household slept.

## Managing idle losses in practice

Idle consumption cannot be eliminated — the inverter must stay on to
deliver power — but it can be managed:

- **Right-size the unit.** The most reliable lever. A correctly sized
  inverter spends its life in the efficient band *and* carries less fixed
  overhead.
- **Use power-saving or eco modes where available.** Many units can drop
  into a low-consumption sleep state and wake on load detection. Verify
  the wake threshold before relying on it: small continuous loads below
  the threshold (a router, a freezer compressor on thermostat) may not
  wake the unit, or may cause it to cycle annoyingly.
- **Switch the inverter off when genuinely unused.** A weekend cabin or a
  seasonal pump site has no reason to carry 24/7 idle draw. A remote
  on/off switch makes the habit realistic; an inverter you have to walk
  to the battery room to switch off stays on.
- **Keep DC-native loads on DC.** A 12V/24V compressor fridge, USB
  lighting and DC water pumps skip the conversion entirely. Every load
  that never passes through the inverter is a load that costs no
  conversion loss and no idle time.
- **Audit phantom loads once with a meter.** Switch everything off and
  read what still flows. In small systems, that reading is often the
  single largest unexplained load in the energy budget.

## What to ask a supplier before comparing datasheets

When evaluating inverter offers, three questions separate honest
efficiency data from marketing:

1. **"What is the efficiency at 10%, 25% and 50% load?"** — not just the
   peak. A supplier who can answer with a curve or a table is describing
   a tested design; a supplier who repeats the headline number is
   describing a datasheet.
2. **"What is the idle or standby consumption in watts?"** — and whether
   the figure is measured with the display, fans and communication ports
   active.
3. **"How does efficiency change with battery voltage?"** — the same unit
   is measurably more efficient at 48V than at 12V for the same power,
   because input current halves and with it the conduction losses. This
   is one more argument for 48V on any system above roughly 2 kW (see our
   system voltage guide).

## A worked comparison

Consider a cabin load profile: 500W average for 6 evening hours, 100W
average overnight for 8 hours, inverter on 24 hours a day.

- **Option A: a 5000W unit, 88% effective at light load, 30W idle.**
  Evening conversion loss: 500W × 6h × 12% ≈ 360Wh. Overnight loss:
  100W × 8h × 15% ≈ 120Wh. Idle: 30W × 24h = 720Wh. Total daily
  conversion overhead ≈ 1.2 kWh.
- **Option B: a 2500W unit, 92% effective at the same loads, 15W idle.**
  Evening loss: 500W × 6h × 8% ≈ 240Wh. Overnight loss: 100W × 8h ×
  12% ≈ 96Wh. Idle: 15W × 24h = 360Wh. Total ≈ 0.7 kWh.

Same loads, same battery bank — the better-matched unit returns roughly
0.5 kWh per day, about 180 kWh per year, to the loads instead of losing
it as heat. On a winter array that is already marginal, that difference
is frequently the difference between a system that runs and a system that
shuts down on overload of the battery bank's lowest SoC in January.
