Pure sine wave and modified sine wave are the two output waveforms you will
see on any inverter spec sheet. The difference looks technical, but it decides
which appliances work correctly — and sometimes whether they work at all.

## What each waveform means

**Modified sine wave** (often called quasi-sine) is a stepped approximation
of a sine wave. It is cheaper to produce and fine for resistive loads like
incandescent lights, heaters and basic tools.

**Pure sine wave** reproduces the smooth sinusoidal AC that comes from the
utility grid. Motors, electronics with sensitive controllers, and anything
with a transformer behave the way they were designed to.

## Which loads need pure sine wave?

| Load type | Modified sine | Pure sine |
|---|---|---|
| Incandescent lights, heaters | Works | Works |
| Induction motors (fans, pumps) | May overheat, noisy, lower efficiency | Works normally |
| Refrigerators / freezers | Risk of compressor damage | Recommended |
| TVs, computers, chargers | May malfunction or run hot | Recommended |
| Medical devices | Not recommended | Recommended |
| Microwave ovens | Slower heating, reduced power | Works at full power |
| Variable-speed / inverter appliances | Can damage electronics | Recommended |

## Practical consequences

- **Motors and compressors** run hotter and less efficiently on modified
  sine wave because the stepped waveform adds harmonics and heat.
- **Sensitive electronics** can reset, hum, or in the worst case fail — the
  dirty waveform can interfere with control boards.
- **Audio equipment** may carry audible mains hum.

## What to choose

For anything beyond simple resistive loads — fridges, pumps, computers, TVs,
medical devices — a **pure sine wave inverter** is the safe default. The price
difference has narrowed significantly in recent years, and the reliability
difference on real projects is large.
