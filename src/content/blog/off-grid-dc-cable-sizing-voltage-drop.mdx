Ask an installer why a system underperforms and you will hear about panel
orientation, battery age, or a shady afternoon. Ask the same question six
months later, after the low-voltage alarms start, and the answer is often
something far less glamorous: the battery-to-inverter cable was sized by
guesswork. DC cable sizing is the least visible part of an off-grid design
and one of the most consequential — the current on the DC side of a
low-voltage inverter is enormous compared to its AC output, and a cable
that is "probably fine" becomes a source of wasted energy, nuisance
alarms, and in the worst cases, heat damage.

This guide walks through the two checks every DC cable must pass — voltage
drop and ampacity — with a worked example you can adapt, the voltage
arithmetic that makes 12V systems uniquely demanding, and the
termination details that decide whether a correctly-sized cable actually
performs.

## Why DC side current dwarfs the AC side

An inverter moves power, not current. For a given output wattage, the DC
input current is the output power divided by the battery voltage times
the conversion efficiency — and at low battery voltages, that division
produces brutal numbers:

| Inverter output | At 48V DC | At 24V DC | At 12V DC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 W | ~23 A | ~46 A | ~93 A |
| 3,000 W | ~69 A | ~139 A | ~278 A |
| 5,000 W | ~116 A | ~231 A | ~463 A |

The table assumes ~90% conversion efficiency. Two conclusions follow
directly from it:

1. **The same power moves with four times the current on a 12V bank than
   on a 48V bank.** Current is what forces cable thickness, so a 12V
   system pays four times the copper for the same watts at the same
   voltage-drop target.
2. **The battery-to-inverter run is usually the highest-current circuit
   in the entire installation** — higher than any PV string, higher than
   any DC load branch. It deserves the most sizing attention, not the
   least.

This is also why our previous [system voltage comparison](/blog/12v-24v-48v-inverter-system/)
keeps returning to cable cost: at 3kW and above, the DC copper itself
starts steering the voltage decision.

## The two checks: voltage drop and ampacity

Every DC cable must pass two independent tests. Passing one does not
excuse failing the other.

**Check 1 — Ampacity (heat).** The conductor must carry the continuous
current without overheating its insulation. Rated ampacity depends on
cable type, insulation temperature rating, installation method (free
air, conduit, bundled with other cables) and ambient temperature. As a
safety floor, many installers work to roughly 5–10 A per mm² of copper
cross-section for short runs in free air, then derate for bundling and
enclosure. If the cable cannot carry the current safely, no voltage-drop
argument rescues it — undersized copper on a high-current circuit can
progress to insulation melt.

**Check 2 — Voltage drop (performance).** Current flowing through cable
resistance lowers the voltage at the far end. On the battery-to-inverter
link this matters twice: the inverter needs supply voltage to produce AC,
and an inverter-charger pushing charge current back into the battery
sees the same resistance in reverse. Practical drop targets:

| DC link | Typical design target |
| --- | --- |
| Battery to inverter | ≤ 2% (some designers go tighter for inverter-chargers) |
| PV string to charge controller | ≤ 2–3% |
| DC load branches | 2–3% |
| Non-critical lighting | up to 5% |

The battery link gets the strictest target because it sits in both
directions of energy flow — discharge and charging — and because the
inverter's low-voltage protection sits right behind it.

In practice, at low voltages and realistic run lengths, **voltage drop
decides the cable size far more often than ampacity does**. Heat sets a
floor; distance raises the bar.

## The voltage drop formula, plainly

For a copper cable, the round-trip drop in volts is:

`V_drop = 2 × ρ × L × I / A`

where `ρ` is copper resistivity (about 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C), `L` is
the **one-way** cable length in meters, `I` the continuous DC current in
amps, and `A` the conductor cross-section in mm². The factor of 2
accounts for the return conductor — forgetting it is the classic sizing
error, and it makes a cable appear half the size it needs to be.

Rearranged for the required cross-section:

`A = 2 × ρ × L × I / V_drop_allowed`

Convert your allowed drop percentage to volts first: 2% of a 24V bank is
0.48V; 2% of 12V is only 0.24V. That shrinking allowance is exactly why
low-voltage systems demand disproportionate copper.

Two temperature notes that matter in real enclosures: copper resistance
rises roughly 20% from 20 °C to 70 °C, so a cable running warm drops
more voltage than the cold number suggests; and sizing everything at
room temperature quietly flatters the result. Add margin rather than
pretending the cable will stay cool.

## Worked example: 3,000 W inverter on a 24V bank

A customer runs a 3,000 W inverter (continuous duty) from a 24V battery
bank placed 2.5 m away. Design to 2% drop.

1. **DC input current:** 3,000 W ÷ (24 V × 0.90) ≈ **139 A**
2. **Allowed drop:** 2% × 24 V = **0.48 V**
3. **Required area:** `2 × 0.0175 × 2.5 × 139 / 0.48` ≈ **25.3 mm²**
4. **Round up** to the next standard size: **35 mm²** copper.

Cross-check ampacity: 139 A on 35 mm² is about 4 A/mm² — comfortably
inside the 5–10 A/mm² safety band for a short, well-ventilated run.
Voltage drop drove the selection here, exactly as expected.

Now run the same example on a 48V bank: current halves to ~69 A, the
allowed drop doubles to 0.96V, and the required area falls to roughly
6.3 mm² — a 10 mm² cable passes with margin. **Same inverter, same
distance, about one-third of the copper.** That is the 48V dividend in
a single calculation.

And the cautionary variant: a builder runs the 12V version of this
inverter and buys 35 mm² "to be safe". Current is ~278 A, allowed drop
0.24V, required area ≈ **101 mm²**. The 35 mm² cable drops roughly 4.3%
— double the target — and the inverter now sees battery voltage minus a
half-volt under load. The symptoms downstream: low-voltage alarms during
surges, chargers backing off early, and batteries that never quite reach
full charge because the charge voltage at the battery terminals is
depressed by cable loss on the way in.

## Symptoms of an undersized DC cable

The failure mode is insidious because every symptom looks like a
different fault:

- **Low-voltage alarms or shutdowns under heavy load** that never appear
  at light load. The battery is fine; the voltage sag happens in the
  cable, not the cells.
- **Charging that stalls below target.** An inverter-charger regulating
  to, say, 28.4V absorption will push that voltage through the same
  cable resistance; the battery terminals see less, absorption ends
  early, and the bank chronically undercharges.
- **Warm or hot cables and lugs** under sustained load — the ampacity
  check being violated in real time.
- **Surge events that trip the inverter offline** even though the
  battery bank has plenty of capacity. Motor-start current through
  cable resistance magnifies the sag beyond the inverter's cutoff
  (see our [surge power guide](/blog/inverter-surge-power-motor-starting/)
  for the load side of this problem).

The diagnostic is cheap: measure voltage directly at the inverter's DC
terminals under full load and compare with the battery terminals. The
difference is your real voltage drop — no math required. More than 2%
under continuous rated load means the cable is the bottleneck.

## Termination: where correctly-sized cables still fail

Sizing the copper is half the job. The termination is where real
installations lose the margin you just calculated:

- **Match the terminal hardware to the conductor.** A 35 mm² cable
  compressed into a lug sized for 25 mm², or a crimp made with the
  wrong die, adds resistance precisely at the point of highest current.
- **Torque the DC terminals properly.** Loose inverter DC terminals
  heat, oxidize, and loosen further — a self-accelerating loop. Recheck
  torque after the first weeks of thermal cycling.
- **Equal-length battery interconnects.** In a series-parallel bank,
  unequal interconnect lengths distribute current unequally between
  parallel strings; keep every interconnect the same length and gauge.
- **Fuse the cable at the battery, sized to the conductor.** The fuse
  protects the cable, not the inverter — a 200A fuse on cable rated for
  139 A continuous protects nothing.
- **Respect the inverter's terminal range.** Our inverters specify
  recommended cable cross-sections in their manuals for exactly this
  reason; the DC input accepts 12V to 72V configurations across the
  off-grid series, and the current at 12V is a different world from the
  current at 48V for the same model.

## Commissioning checklist

- [ ] Cable size calculated from actual current (power ÷ voltage ÷ efficiency), not estimated from inverter model number
- [ ] Voltage drop verified by measurement: battery terminals vs inverter DC terminals at continuous rated load, ≤ 2%
- [ ] Ampacity confirmed for the installation method (bundled? conduit? ambient temperature?)
- [ ] Correct lug size and crimp die for every DC termination
- [ ] DC fuse or breaker at the battery end, rated to protect the conductor
- [ ] Battery interconnects equal length and equal gauge
- [ ] Terminal torque checked, then rechecked after first thermal cycle
- [ ] Surge behavior verified: worst motor start does not trip the inverter's low-voltage cutoff
