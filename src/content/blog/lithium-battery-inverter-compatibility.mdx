On paper, connecting a lithium battery to an inverter is simple: match the
nominal voltage, size the cables, close the breaker. In practice, a battery
that "fits" can still drain incorrectly, shut down mid-discharge, or report a
state of charge that jumps around — because three separate layers of
compatibility all have to line up at once.

This guide walks through those three layers — voltage window, charge profile
and BMS communication — so you can qualify a battery-inverter pair *before*
it ships, not after it fails on a customer's wall.

## Layer 1: the voltage window

A "48 V" battery is not really 48 V. A 48 V LiFePO4 pack sweeps from roughly
58.4 V at full charge down to about 40 V near empty, and a 12 V pack sweeps
from 14.6 V to around 10 V. Your inverter's DC input window has to cover the
*entire* sweep, not just the nominal point.

Two failure modes show up when the window is wrong:

- **Over-voltage cutoff.** If the pack charges above the inverter's maximum
  DC input, the inverter trips or faults every time the battery reaches full
  charge — right when the system should be quietest.
- **Under-voltage shutdown.** If the inverter's low-DC cutoff sits above the
  battery's own empty voltage, the inverter shuts down while usable energy
  remains in the pack. The customer sees this as "the battery dies at 30%".

The practical rule: read both datasheets and confirm the battery's full
charge voltage sits below the inverter's maximum DC input, and the battery's
protection cutoff sits above the inverter's minimum. Leave 1–2 V of margin on
each side.

## Layer 2: the charge profile

Voltage compatibility gets the system running; the charge profile decides
whether the battery lives out its rated cycle life.

| Parameter | Where it's set | What goes wrong if ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption / charge voltage | Inverter charger menu | Set too high, the BMS disconnects on over-voltage; set too low, the bank never reaches 100% |
| Charge current limit | Battery BMS → inverter | Current above the cell rating overheats the pack and ages it prematurely |
| Float voltage | Inverter charger menu | Holding lithium at lead-acid float voltages keeps cells at high stress for nothing |
| Temperature cutoff | Battery BMS | Charging below 0 °C plates lithium onto the anode — permanent capacity loss |

The classic mistake is leaving the inverter's factory defaults, which are
usually tuned for lead-acid: a 13.8 V float on a "12 V" bank, for example.
LiFePO4 wants no float at all — it wants charge, then rest. If your inverter
menu exposes a lithium battery type, select it; if not, set absorption and
float to the values printed on the battery's datasheet and verify them with a
meter after commissioning.

## Layer 3: BMS communication

Modern lithium packs carry a battery management system that monitors cell
voltages, current and temperature, and that BMS can talk to the inverter over
**CAN bus** or **RS485**. When the link is live, the inverter receives
continuous instructions: the exact charge voltage, the current limits *right
now* (which shrink when cells are cold or imbalanced), the true state of
charge, and alarms.

When the link is absent or mismatched, the inverter falls back to fixed
manual settings — and four symptoms follow:

1. **SOC drift.** The display shows 60% while the load dies, because the
   inverter is guessing from voltage instead of reading the BMS.
2. **Charge rejection.** The BMS limits current for a thermal reason; the
   inverter, unaware, pushes full current until the BMS disconnects.
3. **Mid-use shutdowns.** A protection event fires but never reaches the
   inverter, so the system cuts out with no warning or logged fault.
4. **Silent alarms.** High-temperature or over-current flags stay invisible
   in the monitoring app.

The subtle trap: a CAN plug that physically fits does not mean the protocols
match. CAN defines how bits move on the wire; the *message map* — which
register holds the SOC, which holds the charge limit — is where brands
diverge. Always confirm the battery's protocol profile is explicitly
supported by the inverter, and use the manufacturer's pinout, not a
visually identical off-the-shelf cable.

### Communication vs. manual settings

| Aspect | BMS communication | Manual voltage settings |
|---|---|---|
| State of charge | Read directly from BMS | Estimated from voltage |
| Current limits | Dynamic, follows temperature | Fixed values |
| Protection response | Coordinated warning then shutdown | Inverter trips blindly |
| Warranty position | Normally required for lithium | Often excluded |

For any customer-facing installation, treat communication as required, not
optional. Manual settings are acceptable for bench testing or emergency
substitution with conservative limits.

## A pre-purchase checklist

Run this list before issuing a purchase order for a battery-inverter
combination:

- [ ] Battery full-charge voltage < inverter maximum DC input (with ≥ 1 V margin)
- [ ] Battery protection cutoff > inverter minimum DC input (with ≥ 1 V margin)
- [ ] Inverter max charge current ≤ battery BMS continuous charge rating
- [ ] Inverter max discharge current ≤ battery BMS continuous discharge rating
- [ ] Protocol profile (CAN or RS485) explicitly listed as supported by both sides
- [ ] Communication cable pinout per manufacturer documentation
- [ ] Charge parameters available in writing (absorption, float, cutoffs)
- [ ] One bench test: full charge, full discharge, SOC display tracked at both ends

The last item costs an afternoon and prevents the most expensive class of
field failure — a system that works at the warehouse and misbehaves on the
customer's wall.
